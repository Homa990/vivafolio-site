The Duke Who Would Not Pass On the Fire

Yínghuò (熒惑), “the dazzling deluder”: the planet Mars

Chinese The state of Song, in the plains of central China (today’s Henan) · 5 min read

In ancient China, the sky was not just beautiful. It was a government.

Every part of it watched over a part of the Earth. The court astronomers sat up all night on their towers, writing down every comet, every eclipse, every wandering of the planets, because Heaven was always speaking, and a wise ruler had to listen.

And of all the lights in the sky, the one they watched most nervously was red.

They called it Yinghuo, the dazzling deluder. It glowed like an ember. It moved forward through the stars, then stopped, then went backwards, then forward again, as if it could not make up its mind, or as if it were searching for someone. Wherever it lingered, the astronomers said, trouble would follow: war, rebellion, a ruler’s death.

There was one place in the sky where it was most feared. The Chinese called it Xin, the Heart: three stars in the middle of the great Azure Dragon, and in the centre of them a red star of its own, the Great Fire, the star we call Antares. When the red planet came to stand still beside the red heart, two fires burning side by side, it was the worst omen in the sky.

About two and a half thousand years ago, in the small state of Song, it happened. And the Heart was the very part of the sky that watched over Song.

Duke Jing of Song was afraid. He called his astrologer, a man named Zi Wei, and asked him what it meant.

Zi Wei did not soften it. Yinghuo is Heaven’s punishment, he said, and the Heart is the star of Song. The disaster will fall on you, my lord.

Then he lowered his voice. But it can be moved. There are ways. You could shift it onto your chief minister.

The duke said: My chief minister is the one who helps me govern the country. To send death to him in my place would bring no luck.

Then you could shift it onto the people.

The duke said: If the people die, whose ruler will I be? I would rather die alone.

Then you could shift it onto the harvest, onto the year itself.

The duke said: If the harvest fails, the people will starve, and if they starve, they will die. What kind of ruler kills his own people to save his own life? Who would call me ruler then? No. My life has simply come to its end. Say no more.

Zi Wei stood up. He walked out of the room, then turned, faced north, and bowed twice to the floor. My lord, let me congratulate you, he said. Heaven lives high, but it hears what is said below. Three times you have spoken words of the highest virtue, and three times Heaven will reward you. Tonight Yinghuo will move three lodges away. And your life will be lengthened by twenty-one years.

How can you know that? asked the duke.

And Zi Wei explained: three good sayings, three rewards; three lodges of the sky, each lodge seven stars, each star a year. Three sevens are twenty-one.

That night, the book says, the astronomers went up on their tower and watched. And Yinghuo had moved three lodges away.

That is where the old book ends. But history added a second chapter, and it is darker.

Nearly five hundred years later, in 7 BC, the astronomers of the Han emperor reported that the red planet was again standing in the Heart. This emperor did not say no. His chief minister, Zhai Fangjin, was made to take the blame, and was ordered to take his own life. He did. And a few weeks later, the emperor died anyway.

Here is the strangest part. Modern astronomers can now run the planets backwards, and they have checked the old records. Mars really does come to the Heart now and then, dozens of times over the centuries. But a historian of astronomy, Huang Yi-long, found that most of the times it was written down in the official histories, it never happened at all. Even the omen of 7 BC seems not to have happened as it was reported. Some of the sky’s warnings were written by people, not by Heaven.

So which story is true? Perhaps neither, exactly. But you can see why the Chinese kept telling the first one, and remembered the second as a warning. The sky was supposed to test rulers. One ruler passed the test by refusing to hand his danger to anyone else. Another failed it, and the danger found him all the same.

Next time you see a red light that does not twinkle, wandering near the red heart of the Scorpion, remember Duke Jing. When trouble comes, there is always a voice offering to send it to someone else. He said no, three times.

Find it tonight

Look for a star that is plainly orange-red and does not twinkle: that is Yinghuo. To see the Heart lodge of the story, find Antares, the red heart of the Scorpion, low in the south on summer evenings (from the northern half of the world). When Mars passes near it, two red lights stand side by side. The Greeks named Antares “the rival of Ares” for exactly that reason.

Who tells this story

The scholars and court astronomers of ancient China, for whom the sky was a mirror of the government: each part of the sky watched over a state on Earth, and the movements of the planets were Heaven’s comments on how the rulers behaved. The story of Duke Jing of Song is set in the 5th century BC and was written down some two hundred years later as a lesson in good rule. Even in ancient times, the philosopher Wang Chong doubted that Heaven could be moved by words.

Told in The Annals of Lü Buwei (Lüshi Chunqiu, about 239 BC), and again in the Huainanzi and the Grand Scribe’s Records.
