A tale of The ancient Greeks · Orion

The Hunter and the Scorpion

Ōríōn (Ὠρίων), the giant hunter

Greek Greece, Chios and Crete · 4 min read

On a winter night, in the middle of the sky, a giant is walking. You cannot miss him: two bright stars for his shoulders, two for his knees and feet, and across his waist a belt of three stars in a perfect line, so neat that it hardly looks natural. He has been walking across the winter sky for as long as people have looked up.

And yet, if you wait for summer and look for him again, he is gone. In his place, low in the south, a scorpion has risen, with a red star glowing at its heart. The two never share the sky. The Greeks knew why.

The giant’s name was Orion. He was the son of Poseidon, god of the sea, and his father gave him a strange gift: he could walk through the ocean as if it were a meadow, with his head and shoulders above the highest waves. He grew taller than any man and stronger than any beast, and he became the greatest hunter the world had ever known.

His strength brought him glory, and it brought him trouble. On the island of Chios, the king, Oenopion, promised Orion his daughter Merope if Orion would rid the island of its wild beasts. Orion hunted down every lion and wolf on the island. But the king kept finding reasons not to give his daughter away, and one night, when Orion had drunk too much of the king’s wine, the king’s men crept up on the sleeping giant and put out his eyes. Then they left him on the seashore, blind.

Orion did not give up. He heard the hammering of a blacksmith’s forge, felt his way toward it, and picked up a boy who worked there, Cedalion. He set the boy on his shoulders. “Be my eyes,” he said. “Guide me to the place where the sun comes up.”

And so the blind giant walked east, across the land and through the sea, with a child on his shoulders calling out the way. At the very edge of the world, where the sun rises out of the ocean every morning, he turned his empty eyes toward the light. The first rays of the sun touched his face, and his sight came back.

Now he could see again, and he was more restless than ever. He went to the island of Crete and hunted there with Artemis, the goddess of the hunt, and her mother Leto. Day after day they ran through the mountains together. No deer was too fast for him, no boar too fierce.

That was when Orion said too much.

“There is no creature on this Earth,” he boasted, “that I cannot kill. Give me time, and I will hunt down every one of them.”

The Earth heard him.

The Earth was the mother of every living thing, every lion and hare and bird, and she was not going to let one hunter empty her of her children. From a crack in the ground she sent a creature unlike any Orion had ever faced: not a lion, not a bear, but a scorpion. A giant scorpion, armoured, silent, with a sting that could kill a giant.

Orion turned and lifted his club. But the club was useless against the scorpion’s hard shell, and it slipped beneath his blows and struck. The sting went into his heel. The greatest hunter in the world fell down, and did not get up again.

Artemis and Leto grieved for their companion. They asked Zeus to remember him, and Zeus lifted Orion into the sky and made him a constellation, with his club raised and his belt of three stars shining at his waist.

But Zeus lifted the scorpion into the sky too, as a reminder of what happens to those who boast that they can conquer the whole Earth.

Then the two were set on opposite sides of the heavens, as far apart as the sky allows, and there they still are. When the Scorpion climbs over the eastern horizon in summer, Orion is already sinking in the west, hurrying away. When Orion returns to the winter sky, the Scorpion has gone down. They chase each other around the world, year after year, and the hunter never has to face that sting again.

So the next time you see the three stars of the belt, look a little lower, at the bright blue-white star at Orion’s foot. Its name, Rigel, comes from the Arabic word for “foot”. Somewhere down there, long ago, the sting went in, and the hunter has walked carefully ever since.

Find it tonight

In November Orion rises in the east around 9 or 10 pm and by 2 am stands high in the south. From December to March he is up all evening: look for three bright stars in a short straight line, the belt, between red Betelgeuse at his shoulder and blue-white Rigel at his foot. In the southern hemisphere he stands upside down, and the belt and sword make “the Saucepan”.

Who tells this story

The ancient Greeks, in a myth with many versions. In some, Artemis herself kills Orion, tricked by her brother Apollo; in others, he is killed for loving the dawn goddess Eos. The version told here, with the scorpion sent by the Earth, is the one most often linked to the stars, and it explains why the two constellations are never seen together.

Told since at least the time of Hesiod (about 700 BC); collected by later writers such as pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hyginus and Ovid.
