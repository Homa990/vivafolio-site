A tale of The Lakota · Orion

The Chief’s Hand

Nape, the Hand: Orion’s belt is the wrist, Rigel and the sword make the fingers and thumb

Lakota The northern Great Plains · 3 min read

Turn Orion upside down in your mind, forget the hunter, and look again at the bottom half of him. The three stars of the belt become a wrist. The bright star below becomes a fingertip, the faint stars around it a palm, the sword hanging down a thumb. It is a hand, an enormous hand, reaching across the winter sky.

The Lakota see it, and they know whose hand it is.

There was once a chief, a powerful man, with a fine lodge and many horses and more than he could ever need. But he was a selfish man. When others went hungry, he kept what he had. When it was time to give, as a good leader must give, he gave nothing. Among the Lakota, where a person’s worth is measured by what they share, this was a great wrong.

The powers above saw it, and they decided that the chief must be taught.

The Thunder beings came down. They are the great beings of the storm, the ones whose wings make the thunder and whose eyes flash as lightning. They seized the chief, and they tore off his arm. And they did not leave it on the Earth. They carried the chief’s arm up into the sky and placed it there, where everyone could see it, night after night: the hand that would not give.

The chief was broken. His daughter, who loved him, could not bear to see him that way. She made a promise in front of all the people. “Whoever brings back my father’s arm,” she said, “I will marry him.”

Many young men heard her. But how could any man climb into the sky?

There was one who could. His name was Fallen Star. His mother was a Lakota woman and his father was a star, and he had been born between the two worlds, so the road between the Earth and the sky was open to him as to no one else.

How he went up and how he won the arm back, the tellers tell in different ways. But all of them agree on what happened next. Fallen Star brought the arm home. He returned it to the chief, and the chief was whole again, and humbled. And Fallen Star married the chief’s daughter, as she had promised.

Yet the Hand is still in the sky, because the lesson is still there to be learned.

Watch it through the year. In autumn and winter, the Hand is with us, crossing the sky every night. But in spring, as the evenings grow warm, it sinks lower in the west after sunset, until one evening it is gone. For the Lakota, the time when the Hand leaves the sky belongs to a season of giving and sacrifice, when people offer something of themselves so that life and the Earth will be renewed. The selfish hand disappears, and in its place the people give.

When it comes back in the autumn, reaching up over the eastern hills, you can look at it and remember the chief, and the daughter who kept her promise, and the star-born boy who went where no one else could go. And you can ask yourself the question the Hand has always asked: what is in my hand, and who is it for?

Find it tonight

In November the Hand rises in the east late in the evening. In winter it is up all evening in the south. In early spring it sinks into the west after sunset and disappears for the summer, which in Lakota star knowledge is part of its meaning.

Show me these stars

Who tells this story

The Lakota, one of the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ (the Seven Council Fires), whose star knowledge links the sky to the land and to their ceremonies through the year. Tellers differ on the details, such as whether the arm was taken by the Thunder beings or by the powers above, and on exactly which stars make the thumb.

Lakota star knowledge, gathered with Lakota elders and published by Sinte Gleska University (Ronald Goodman, Lakota Star Knowledge, 1990).

With respect: Lakota star knowledge is connected to ceremony. This retelling keeps to what Lakota scholars and elders have published for everyone to read.
