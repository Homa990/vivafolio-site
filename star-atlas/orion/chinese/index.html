A tale of The Chinese · Orion

The Brothers Who Could Never Meet

Shēn (參), “the Three”: the belt stars of Orion, set opposite Shāng (商), the red heart of the Scorpion

Chinese Ancient China: Kuanglin, Shangqiu and Daxia · 4 min read

In a palace at Kuanglin, long ago, there lived an emperor named Gaoxin, and he had two sons. The elder was called Ebo. The younger was called Shichen.

They had the same father. They grew up under the same roof. They ate at the same table. And from the very beginning, they could not stand each other.

It was not a quarrel. A quarrel ends. This was something else: a fire that never went out. If one brother said north, the other said south. If one stood, the other sat. As boys they fought with words, and as young men they fought with fists, and when they were grown, princes with armies of their own, they fought with spears and shields. The old chronicle says that day after day they took up their weapons against each other, and that there was never a single season of peace between them.

The whole country suffered for it. Villages burned in their battles. Fields went unharvested because the farmers had been called to war. And everyone knew that this would never stop, because the two brothers did not want anything. They did not want land, or gold, or the throne. They only wanted to defeat each other.

At last the emperor of the age, the wise Yao, could bear it no longer. He saw that there was only one way to make peace between two people who will never stop fighting: they must never see each other again.

So he sent them away, as far apart as the world allowed.

Ebo, the elder, was sent east, to a place called Shangqiu. He was given a sacred duty: to watch over a great red star, the one the Chinese called the Great Fire, in the heart of the constellation we call the Scorpion, and to make the offerings to it. Ebo’s star became known as Shang.

Shichen, the younger, was sent west, to a place called Daxia. He too was given a star to watch over: a row of three bright stars in a line, the ones we call the belt of Orion. His stars became known as Shen.

And here is the cleverness of it. The two stars, the red Fire and the three in a row, stand on exactly opposite sides of the sky.

Think of it the way the old astronomers did. When the Fire of Shang rises in the east at nightfall, the three stars of Shen are already sinking in the west. When Shen climbs back into the evening sky, Shang has already gone down. All through the year they take turns, one rising as the other sets. In all the nights of all the years since, they have never once been seen together.

The brothers had wanted to defeat each other. Instead, the sky made sure they would never even meet.

The story might have stayed in the old chronicle, a lesson for kings about the price of hatred. But fifteen centuries later, in the year 759, a poet named Du Fu came home to an old friend.

The country was at war. Du Fu had been wandering for years, and so had his friend Wei Ba. They had not seen each other in twenty years. When at last they met again, by lamplight, in a small house, Du Fu could hardly believe it. His friend’s hair had turned grey. Wei Ba had married, and his children, whom Du Fu had never met, came running to bow politely to the stranger and ask where he had come from. Wei Ba sent them out into the night rain to cut spring chives for dinner.

And Du Fu wrote a poem about that evening. It begins with the two brothers:

In this life, people do not get to meet. They drift apart, like Shen and Shang.

He did not mean enemies. He meant friends. He meant everyone who has ever been separated by war, or work, or distance, or time, and has looked up at the night and known that somewhere, someone they love is under the same sky and still cannot be reached. The poem ends with the two friends drinking cup after cup, knowing that tomorrow mountains will lie between them again.

Ever since, in China, when two people who love each other keep missing one another, they say they are like Shen and Shang.

So here is the strange gift of this story. It began with two brothers who hated each other, and it ended as the most tender way of saying I miss you. Tonight, when Orion’s three stars climb out of the east, the red heart of the Scorpion has already set behind you, on the far side of the world. They will cross the sky again tomorrow, and the night after, always apart.

But anyone who knows the story knows that they are still there, both of them, keeping their turns. And somewhere tonight, someone is looking at the three stars of Shen and thinking of a person they cannot see.

Find it tonight

In November, Orion’s three belt stars climb out of the east around 9 or 10 pm and cross the sky until dawn. Now try to find Antares, the red heart of Scorpius: you can’t. It set in the west soon after sunset. Wait until summer and the two swap places: Antares glows low in the south, and Orion is nowhere to be seen.

Who tells this story

The Chinese, in one of their oldest histories, the Zuo Zhuan. A statesman, Zichan, tells the story to explain an illness of the lord of Jin. The chronicle says only that ‘the later emperor’ separated them; the old commentators say this was the sage-emperor Yao. Some retellings spell the younger brother’s name Shishen. Du Fu’s poem To the Recluse Wei Ba (贈衛八處士) made the pair a byword for friends and loved ones who cannot meet.

Recorded in the Zuo Zhuan (左傳), a chronicle of the 4th century BC, under the first year of Duke Zhao; made immortal by the poet Du Fu in AD 759.
