A tale of The ancient Egyptians · Orion

The Star Who Brings Back the River

Sah (sꜣḥ), the star-god of Orion, followed by Sopdet (spdt), the star Sirius

Egyptian The Nile valley, Egypt · 5 min read

By the end of spring in ancient Egypt, the river was tired. The Nile shrank between its banks, the fields cracked in the heat, and the whole country waited. Everything they ate, everything they built, depended on one thing happening every year: the river had to rise, spill over the land, and leave behind the black mud where wheat would grow.

The Egyptians did not leave this to chance. They watched the sky for a sign. And the sign was a woman made of light, following a giant.

The giant was Sah. He was made of the stars we call Orion, and the Egyptians drew him as a tall man striding through the sky, sometimes standing in a boat, often looking back over his shoulder. In the oldest writings of Egypt he is called “the father of the gods”, and he was one of the most important beings in the whole night.

Behind him walked Sopdet, the brightest star of all, the one we call Sirius. The Egyptians pictured her as a woman crowned with a star, and the two of them belonged together. One old text speaks to her directly: Sopdet, your face is toward Sah.

Every year, something strange happened to Sopdet. In spring she sank lower and lower in the evening twilight until one evening she was gone, swallowed by the glow of the Sun. For about seventy days she could not be seen at all.

Then, one summer morning, just before sunrise, a priest watching the eastern horizon would see her: a single brilliant point of light, flashing low in the dawn for a few moments before the Sun rose and hid her again. Sopdet had come back.

And soon afterwards, the river came back too.

The return of Sopdet was the beginning of the Egyptian year. It was the sign that the waters were coming, that the dry land would drink again, that life would return to the fields. You can see why the Egyptians came to tell it alongside their greatest story, the story of Osiris.

Osiris was a good king, the king who first taught Egypt to farm, and his brother Seth envied him with a hatred that would not sleep.

No single Egyptian papyrus tells the whole story from beginning to end; it lived in hymns, spells and temple rites. But much later, a Greek writer named Plutarch, who had read and asked about it, set it down in one piece. Seth, he wrote, had a magnificent chest made, of rare wood inlaid with gold, exactly the size of his brother’s body. At a feast he showed it to the guests and promised it to whoever fitted inside. One by one they lay down in it; none of them fitted. Then Osiris lay down. In a moment Seth’s men slammed the lid, nailed it shut, sealed it with molten lead, and threw it into the Nile.

The river carried the chest out to sea, and the sea carried it far away, to the shore of Byblos, where it came to rest against a young tamarisk tree. The tree grew around it, enclosing the chest inside its trunk, until it was so tall and splendid that the king of Byblos had it cut down to make a pillar for his palace, never knowing what was inside.

Isis searched everywhere. She cut her hair in mourning and wandered the world, asking everyone she met, even children, until at last she learned where the chest had gone. She went to Byblos, won the friendship of the queen, and asked for one thing only: the pillar. She cut her husband free from the wood and carried him home.

But Seth, hunting by moonlight in the marshes, found the chest. In his fury he tore his brother’s body into pieces and scattered them across Egypt.

So Isis began again. With her sister Nephthys, she sailed the marshes in a little boat made of papyrus, searching every reedbed for every piece, weeping as she went. Piece by piece they found him. With the help of Anubis, the jackal-headed god of embalming, they bound him together in linen, the first of all mummies. And Isis, spreading her wings over him, breathed life back into her husband.

Osiris could not return to the world of the living. But he lived again, as the king of the world beyond death and the power behind everything that grows anew: the grain that sprouts from buried seed, the river that rises every year from its low summer bed. And Isis bore him a son, Horus, whom she hid in the papyrus marshes until he was grown and strong enough to defeat Seth and take back his father’s throne.

Long before Plutarch, in the oldest writings of all, the Egyptians had already joined Osiris to the stars: they saw Sah as the soul of Osiris, and Sopdet as Isis. So when the woman of light rose again in the dawn, faithfully following her husband across the sky, she was doing what she had always done. She had searched, she had found him, she was bringing him back from death once more. And the river and the green fields came back with him.

The kings of Egypt hoped to join them. Deep inside the oldest pyramids, the walls are covered with carved spells, the Pyramid Texts, written to help the king after he died. Many of them promise him a place in the sky. You are this great star, one says to the dead king, the companion of Sah, who crosses the sky with Sah. The king was not meant to vanish into the dark. He was meant to rise, and walk among the stars that never stop returning.

So when you find Orion in the winter sky, follow the line of his belt down and to the left until you reach the most brilliant star of the night. That is Sopdet. Watch her for a while, and you will see her doing what she has always done: walking behind the giant, a little to the east, faithful, patient. For the Egyptians, as long as she kept walking, the river would always come home.

Find it tonight

Find Orion’s belt, then follow its three stars down and to the left: the dazzling white star you reach is Sirius, Sopdet, the brightest star of the night. In November both rise in the east late in the evening. In Egypt, Sirius vanishes into the Sun’s glow in spring and returns in the dawn in midsummer.

Who tells this story

The ancient Egyptians, over three thousand years. Their beliefs changed over time: in the oldest texts Sah is a god in his own right, the “father of the gods”; later he is joined with Osiris, as Sopdet is with Isis. The tale of the chest and the city of Byblos comes from Plutarch, a Greek writer of about AD 100, two thousand years after the Pyramid Texts; older Egyptian writings tell the same story in pieces, in hymns and spells. The famous idea that the Giza pyramids were laid out to match Orion’s belt is a modern theory that Egyptologists do not accept.

From the Pyramid Texts, carved inside royal pyramids around 2400–2300 BC, to the temple calendars of Greek and Roman times.
