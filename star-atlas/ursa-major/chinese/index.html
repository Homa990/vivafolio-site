A tale of The Chinese · The Great Bear

Two Old Men Under the Mulberry Tree

Běidǒu (北斗), the Northern Dipper, the carriage of the Celestial Emperor

Chinese China, in the time of the Three Kingdoms · 4 min read

In old China, the seven bright stars of the north were not a bear or a ladle. They were a carriage. The Celestial Emperor, ruler of the sky, rode in it around the North Star, and as it turned, it marked the seasons: when the handle pointed east, it was spring; south, summer; west, autumn; north, winter. The Northern Dipper, Běidǒu, was the great clock of heaven.

But it kept another kind of time as well. People said that the Northern Dipper held the book in which every person’s death was written. And far away, low in the southern sky, a smaller dipper of six stars, the Southern Dipper, held the book of everyone’s birth and life.

Almost nobody ever met them. But one boy did.

His name was Zhao Yan, and he was a young man, healthy and strong, working in the fields with his family. One day a stranger walked past their farm, stopped, and looked at the boy for a long time. The stranger was Guan Lu, the most famous fortune-teller in all of China, who could read a person’s fate in their face as easily as you read these words.

Guan Lu shook his head sadly. “What a pity,” he said. “This young man will not live past nineteen.”

The boy’s father fell to his knees in the dust. “Please,” he begged. “He is my son. Is there nothing that can be done?”

Guan Lu was quiet for a while. Then he said, “Perhaps. Listen carefully.” And he gave the boy strange instructions.

“Take a jar of good clear wine and a dish of dried venison. On the day I tell you, walk to the great mulberry tree that stands south of the wheat field. Under it you will find two men playing weiqi, the game of black and white stones. Do not say a word. Simply pour the wine into their cups and set the meat beside them. Whenever a cup is empty, fill it again. If they ask you anything, do not answer. Only bow to them, and bow again.”

On the chosen day, Zhao Yan did exactly as he was told. He carried the wine and the venison to the mulberry tree, and there, sitting in its shade, were two old men bent over a game board. One sat on the north side of the board, and one on the south.

They were so lost in their game that they did not look up. Silently, the boy filled their cups. Without thinking, they drank. Silently, he set the meat beside them. Without thinking, they ate. He kept their cups full all afternoon, and the game went on and on.

At last the game was finished. The old man on the north side looked up, saw the boy, and frowned.

“What are you doing here?” he said sharply. The boy said nothing. He only bowed.

Then the old man on the south side smiled. “We have drunk his wine and eaten his food,” he said gently. “We cannot pretend we have not. We owe him something.”

“His fate is already written,” said the man on the north side. “The book is settled.”

“Let me see the book,” said the man on the south side.

He took the register, found the boy’s name, and read: Zhao Yan. Nineteen years. He picked up his brush. With one quick stroke he moved the characters around, so that the record no longer said nineteen, 十九, but ninety, 九十. Then he handed it back.

“There,” he said to the boy. “Now you will live to ninety.”

Zhao Yan bowed one last time, deeply, and ran home through the wheat field to tell his father.

When Guan Lu heard what had happened, he laughed with delight. “Do you know who those two old men were?” he asked. “The one sitting on the north side was the Northern Dipper. The one on the south side was the Southern Dipper. The Southern Dipper writes down our births and our lives. The Northern Dipper writes down our deaths. That is why everyone who prays for a longer life prays to the Northern Dipper.”

And so in China, people say: The Southern Dipper records life, and the Northern Dipper records death. For centuries, people who longed for a long life burned incense to the two stars, and to the seven bright stars of the north, the stern old man who keeps the book.

Look for him tonight, slowly turning his carriage around the North Star, the book of every life tucked under his arm. And if you ever see two old men playing a board game under a tree, you know what to do: say nothing, and keep their cups full.

Find it tonight

In November the Dipper lies low in the north in the evening and climbs the north-east sky through the night. The Southern Dipper (Nándǒu, 南斗) is a smaller ladle of six stars in Sagittarius, low in the south-west on summer evenings.

Who tells this story

The Chinese, in a tale from the Soushen Ji, a famous 4th-century collection of strange stories. Its hero, Guǎn Lù (管輅), was a real diviner of the 3rd century, famous for his predictions. In Chinese tradition the Northern Dipper keeps the register of death and the Southern Dipper the register of life: 南斗注生，北斗注死.

From the Soushen Ji (搜神記, In Search of the Supernatural), collected by Gan Bao in the 4th century AD.
