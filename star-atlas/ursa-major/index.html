One sky, many eyes

The Great Bear

Seven bright stars, easy to find on almost any clear night in the north: a bowl of four and a handle of three. They are not a whole constellation, only the brightest part of the Great Bear, but they are the shape that people everywhere have learned first. Sailors, farmers, travellers and runaways have used them to find north. And because they turn around the North Star like the hand of a giant clock, many peoples have used them to tell the season, the hour, and the story of the year.

One sky, many eyes: its other names

  • Arktos Megalē (Ἄρκτος Μεγάλη) Ancient Greek: “the Great Bear, once the huntress Callisto”
  • Banāt Naʿsh (بنات نعش) Arabic: “the daughters of the bier”
  • Haft Owrang (هفت اورنگ) Persian: “the seven thrones; the poet Jāmī named his seven great poems after it”
  • Běidǒu (北斗) Chinese: “the Northern Dipper, the Celestial Emperor’s carriage”
  • Saptarṣi (सप्तर्षि) Sanskrit, India: “the seven sages; the tiny star beside the middle of the handle is Arundhatī, wife of the sage Vasiṣṭha”
  • Muin Mi’kmaq: “the bear, hunted by seven birds”
  • The Plough British: “for its shape”
  • Charles’s Wain Old English: “the wagon of Charlemagne (or of the peasant, ceorl)”
  • Karlavagnen Swedish: “the man’s wagon”
  • The Drinking Gourd African American songs of the Underground Railroad: “follow it to find the North Star, and freedom”
  • Le Grand Chariot / La Casserole French: “the great wagon, the saucepan”

Find it tonight

In November the Dipper is low in the north in the evening and climbs the north-east sky through the night. From northern Europe and Canada it never sets. South of the equator it is hard to see, low over the northern horizon in autumn evenings (April and May).

What the telescope says

  • Five of the seven Dipper stars are a real family, born together and travelling through space side by side, about 80 light-years away.
  • The two end stars of the bowl point to the North Star: follow them about five times their own distance.
  • Mizar, at the bend of the handle, has a faint companion, Alcor. Seeing both was an old test of good eyesight.
  • Over thousands of years, the stars’ own movements will slowly bend the Dipper out of shape.
