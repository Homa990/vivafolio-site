The Wagon That Drives Around the Pole

Karlsvagn, “the man’s wagon” (Swedish Karlavagnen, Danish Karlsvognen)

Norse Scandinavia and Iceland · 3 min read

Go to Sweden, or Denmark, or Norway, and ask a child to point to the seven bright stars in the north. They will not say “the Great Bear”. They will say Karlavagnen, or Karlsvognen: the man’s wagon.

In the North, those stars were never an animal. They were a vehicle, the most important one in a farming world: a sturdy wooden wagon. The four stars of the bowl are its body, standing on four wheels. The three stars of the handle are the long pole at the front, the shaft to which the horses or oxen are harnessed.

The old Norse language had a word for it, vagn, and the medieval poets of Iceland used it for these stars. Their handbook of poetic language even has a word for the wagon’s road, vagnbraut, the wagon-way, and that road was the sky itself.

It was a fitting picture for the North, where winter nights are endless. Up there, in Iceland, in northern Norway, the seven stars never set. All night long the Wagon drives in a great slow circle around the North Star, its shaft sweeping across the sky like the hand of a clock. In the evening it rolls along the bottom of the circle, close to the horizon; by midnight it has climbed up one side; by morning it stands high overhead, upside down. A farmer who knew the Wagon’s path always knew the hour, even in the middle of a winter that had no daylight at all.

And there was a second, smaller wagon, too. The stars of the Little Bear, the ones around the North Star itself, were called Kvennavagn: the woman’s wagon. The man’s wagon and the woman’s wagon, driving around the top of the world together, side by side, through the long night.

Whose wagon was it? Here the old books fall silent. Some people say it belonged to Thor, the thunder god, who rode across the sky in a chariot pulled by two goats, and whose wheels made the thunder. Others say it was Odin’s. Much later, when Christian Europe told stories of the emperor Charlemagne, some said it was his: Charles’s Wain. But the medieval sources only tell us the man’s wagon, and the man’s name is lost.

Perhaps that is the right ending. It is not a god’s wagon or an emperor’s. It is simply a man’s wagon: the kind any farmer in the North might own, loaded at harvest, creaking home through the dark.

Tonight, when you find the seven stars low in the north, picture it the northern way. Four wheels on the ground, a long shaft reaching forward, and nobody driving. Watch it for an hour and you will see it move, turning slowly around the Pole, on a road that never ends.

Find it tonight

In November evenings the Wagon sits low in the north, its four wheels close to the horizon; through the night it rolls up the north-east. From Scandinavia it never sets: it drives around the Pole all night, every night, all year.

Who tells this story

The Norse and their descendants in Scandinavia and Iceland. The wagon names are well recorded, in medieval Icelandic writings on the calendar and in the poets’ handbook of Snorri Sturluson, and in the living languages of the North. Whose wagon it was is less certain: some modern writers link it to the god Thor and his goat-drawn chariot, or to Odin, but the old sources do not say so clearly, and the name was later explained as Charlemagne’s wagon too.

Named in medieval Icelandic manuscripts; still the everyday name for the Dipper in Swedish, Danish and Norwegian.
