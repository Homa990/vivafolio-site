The Father Who Swallowed His Children

Kronos to the Greeks, Saturnus to the Romans: the planet Saturn

Greek Greece; then Padua, Florence and The Hague · 5 min read

Before the gods of Olympus, there were the Titans, and the king of the Titans was Kronos.

He had taken the throne by force from his own father, the Sky, and he had been warned: one day, one of your own children will do the same to you. Kronos was not going to let that happen. So every time his wife Rhea gave birth, he took the baby from her arms and swallowed it whole. Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, Poseidon: one after another, they disappeared inside their father.

Rhea’s grief, Hesiod says, was beyond bearing. When she was about to give birth for the sixth time, she went to her own parents, the Earth and the Sky, and begged them for a plan. They sent her to the island of Crete, and there, in a cave, she gave birth to a son and hid him. His name was Zeus.

Then she took a large stone, wrapped it in baby’s clothes, and handed it to her husband. Kronos did not even look. He swallowed it down.

Zeus grew up in secret on Crete, and he grew strong. And when the time came, he forced his father to bring up everything he had swallowed. First came the stone, the last thing in. Then, one by one, his brothers and sisters, alive and whole. Together they fought the Titans for ten years, and won, and became the gods of Olympus. Zeus took the stone and set it up at Delphi, Hesiod says, to be a sign thenceforth and a marvel to mortal men.

The Greeks gave the old king a planet: the slowest of the wanderers, the farthest, the palest, the one that takes almost thirty years to go around the sky. Of course it was Kronos. Who else would be so old, so patient, so far away? The Romans called him Saturn.

For two thousand years, nothing more happened to the story. Then, in 1610, Galileo turned his telescope on Saturn.

He expected a round planet, like Jupiter. Instead, he saw something impossible: Saturn seemed to be three bodies, a big one in the middle and two smaller ones pressed against its sides, like ears, or like two servants holding up an old man. Galileo was so astonished, and so afraid someone else would claim the discovery, that he announced it in a scrambled code, a long string of jumbled letters. Unscrambled, it read in Latin: I have observed the highest planet triple.

He watched it for two years. And then, in 1612, he looked again, and the two companions were gone. Saturn was a plain round ball.

Galileo knew his mythology. In a letter written that December, he asked: Have they vanished, then, and fled? Or has Saturn devoured his own children?

He promised they would come back. They did. But Galileo never understood what they were, and nor did anyone else, for forty years. Astronomers drew Saturn with handles, with arms, with ears, with a strange shape like a cup, and nobody could make sense of it.

The answer came from a young Dutchman, Christiaan Huygens, with a better telescope. In 1655 he found a moon going around Saturn, the one we call Titan. And then he understood the ears. He too published his answer as a scrambled code first, and when he unscrambled it, it said: It is surrounded by a thin, flat ring, which nowhere touches it, and which is tilted to the path of the Sun.

A ring. A wide, thin, flat ring around the planet. When we see it tilted, it looks like ears on either side. But every fifteen years or so, as Saturn travels around the Sun, the ring turns edge-on to the Earth. And it is so thin that it almost disappears. That is what happened in 1612. Saturn had not eaten anyone. Galileo had simply been looking at the ring from the side.

It happened again in 2025. If you had looked through a small telescope in March of that year, you would have seen exactly what Galileo saw: a plain ball, with its children gone. Since then the ring has been slowly tilting open again, a little more each year, and it will be widest around 2032.

So find the pale-gold wanderer tonight, the old king, and if you ever have the chance, look at it through a telescope. Most people gasp. It looks like a jewel, too perfect to be real. And somewhere in that gasp is Galileo, four hundred years ago, wondering whether the old god had eaten his children again.

Find it tonight

Saturn is the calm, pale-gold light that does not twinkle, about as bright as the brighter stars. It moves so slowly that it stays in the same part of the zodiac for two or three years. Its rings were edge-on to us in 2025, as they were for Galileo in 1612; they are now slowly opening again, and will be widest around 2032. Any small telescope shows them.

Who tells this story

The myth of Kronos is told by the Greek poet Hesiod in the Theogony, around 700 BC. The Greeks first called the planet Phainon, “the bright one”, and later gave it to Kronos; the Romans matched Kronos with their own Saturnus. The telescope chapter is history: Galileo’s observations of 1610 and 1612 and his letters about them, and Christiaan Huygens’s solution of 1655–1659. Galileo’s question about Saturn devouring his children comes from his letter of December 1612 to Mark Welser.
