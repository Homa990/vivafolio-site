The Star That Counted the Years

Suìxīng (歲星), “the Year Star”: the planet Jupiter

Chinese China, from the Warring States to the empires · 5 min read

How do you count years without a calendar on the wall?

The astronomers of ancient China looked for a clock in the sky, and they found the perfect one: a steady, bright, kingly light that walked through the stars at an even pace, never hurrying, never going too far. It took about twelve years to go all the way around the sky. So they divided the sky into twelve stations, and every year this light moved on to the next.

They called it Suixing, the Year Star. Today we call it Jupiter.

For centuries, a year in China could be named by where the Year Star stood. Old histories say things like in the year when the Year Star was in the Star Record, the way we might say in 1998. The Year Star was the hand of a giant clock, and the face of the clock was the whole sky.

But there were two problems.

The first was the direction. The twelve stations were counted one way around the sky, and the Year Star walked the other way. It was like a clock whose hand runs backwards. So the astronomers did something wonderful: they invented a second Jupiter. An invisible one. A mirror image of the Year Star that walked around the sky in the opposite direction, at exactly the same speed, so that it moved forward through the twelve stations, one each year, in the right order.

They called the invisible planet Taisui, the Great Year. Nobody could see it. It did not exist. And yet, over time, it became one of the most powerful gods in China. Each year has its own Taisui, a general who rules over that year, and some people still visit temples to make peace with the Taisui of the year they were born in. There is an old saying: to dig the earth on Taisui’s head. It means to dare to provoke someone very powerful, because wherever the invisible planet stands, you must never break the ground.

The second problem was smaller, and slower. Jupiter does not take twelve years to go around the sky. It takes eleven years and about ten months. Every year it arrives a little early. After about eighty-six years, the real Year Star had run a whole station ahead of where it was supposed to be. The clock had drifted. Calendar makers had to jump it forward, and in the end the years were counted without looking at Jupiter at all. But the old names, and the invisible god, remained.

There is one more thing in this story, and it is the most mysterious.

In the 4th century BC, more than two thousand years ago, an astronomer named Gan De wrote down what he saw when he looked at the Year Star. Most of his books are lost, but a few lines survive, copied into a later collection. In one of them, describing Jupiter rising in the dawn, he writes that it was very large and bright, and then: as if there were a small reddish star attached to its side.

For two thousand years, nobody made much of that sentence.

Then, in January 1610, in Padua, an Italian professor named Galileo Galilei pointed a new instrument, a telescope he had built himself, at Jupiter. Beside the planet he saw three little stars in a line. He assumed they were ordinary stars. But the next night they had moved. And the night after that, they had moved again. Within a week he had found a fourth. They were not stars at all. They were moons, going around Jupiter, the way our Moon goes around the Earth. It was the first proof that not everything in the sky goes around us. Galileo rushed his discovery into print in a little book called The Starry Messenger.

In 1981, a Chinese historian of astronomy, Xi Zezong, read Gan De’s sentence again and asked a bold question. Could it be that Gan De had seen one of those moons, without any telescope, two thousand years before Galileo? The largest of Jupiter’s moons, Ganymede, is bright enough to be seen by a very sharp eye, if Jupiter’s glare is hidden, say, behind a tree branch or the edge of a roof.

Nobody can prove it. The colour is a puzzle: the moons do not look red. But nobody can quite rule it out either.

So tonight, find Jupiter, the brightest steady light in the sky. Borrow a pair of binoculars, rest them on a wall to keep them still, and look. If the sky is clear, you will see what Galileo saw: a few tiny points of light in a line beside the planet. Come back tomorrow, and they will have moved. And as you look, think of Gan De, on a cold morning more than two thousand years ago, squinting at the Year Star and writing down, very carefully, that something small and faint was keeping it company.

Find it tonight

Jupiter is the big, steady, creamy-white light that is often the brightest point in the night sky (after Venus and the Moon). It moves about one sign of the zodiac each year. With binoculars held very still, or resting on a wall, look for the tiny points of light in a line beside it: these are its four great moons.

Who tells this story

The astronomers of ancient China, who kept some of the longest and most careful records of the sky in the world. The Year Star calendar and Taisui are well documented. The claim that Gan De saw one of Jupiter’s moons with the naked eye was made by the historian of astronomy Xi Zezong in 1981, from a single sentence; it is possible but uncertain, and the moon’s “reddish” colour is hard to explain. Whether the twelve animals of the Chinese zodiac began with Jupiter’s twelve steps is still argued by historians.

The Year Star is named in the Zuozhuan (4th century BC) and in the Grand Scribe’s Records (about 90 BC); Gan De’s note survives in the Kaiyuan Treatise on Astrology (8th century AD).
