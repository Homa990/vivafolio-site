A tale of The ancient Greeks · Mercury

The Baby Who Was Too Quick for the Gods

Hermes to the Greeks, Mercurius to the Romans: the planet Mercury

Greek Mount Kyllene in Arcadia, and the hills of Pieria, Greece · 5 min read

Of all the gods of Greece, Hermes was born in the greatest hurry.

His mother was Maia, a shy nymph who lived in a cave on Mount Kyllene, far from the other gods. His father was Zeus. And the old poem tells his first day like this: Born with the dawning, at mid-day he played on the lyre, and in the evening he stole the cattle of far-shooting Apollo.

That was just day one.

He was barely out of his cradle when he wandered to the mouth of the cave and met a tortoise, walking slowly through the grass. Hello, lovely thing, said the baby god, and what a lucky meeting for me. He took it inside, scooped out its shell, stretched seven strings of sheep gut across it, and fixed two arms and a crossbar to it. He had invented the lyre. He tried a few notes. They were beautiful. He sang a little song to go with them, about his own mother and father, as babies do.

But by afternoon he was hungry. Not for milk. For meat.

So as the Sun went down he climbed out of the cave and ran north, all the way to the hills of Pieria, where the gods kept their cattle. These cows belonged to his big brother, Apollo, the god of the Sun’s light and music. Hermes cut fifty of them out of the herd. And then he did something very clever. He drove them backwards, so their hoofprints pointed the wrong way. And he wove himself a pair of sandals out of twigs and leaves, big, strange, shapeless shoes, so that no one could tell whose feet had walked there.

On the way, one old man, working in his vineyard, saw him pass. Old man, said Hermes, you have seen nothing, and you have heard nothing. Remember that.

By the river Alpheus he rubbed two sticks together and invented the way to make fire. He roasted two of the cows. Then, before dawn, he slipped back home to Kyllene, through the keyhole of the cave, the poem says, like the autumn breeze, like mist. He climbed into his cradle, pulled his blanket up to his chin, and held his lyre, the picture of an innocent baby.

His mother was not fooled. Where have you been, you little rascal, at this time of night?

Meanwhile Apollo had found his cows missing. He followed the tracks, which went the wrong way. He asked the old man in the vineyard, who remembered something after all. He read the flight of a bird. And at last he came to the cave on Kyllene and stood over the cradle.

Little one, lying in your cradle, said Apollo, tell me where my cows are, or I will throw you down into the darkness under the earth.

Hermes opened his eyes very wide. Son of Leto, what harsh words. Do I look like a cattle thief? I was born yesterday. All I care about is sleep and my mother’s milk and warm baths. I don’t even know what cows are. I have only heard of them.

Apollo laughed despite himself, picked the baby up, and carried him off to Mount Olympus to be judged by their father.

In front of Zeus and all the gods, Hermes told his story again, with great feeling. I am a truthful person, he said, and I do not know how to tell a lie. Zeus laughed out loud at his naughty child, and told the two brothers to go and find the cows together, and to stop quarrelling.

They found them. Apollo was still angry. So Hermes took out his lyre and began to play.

And Apollo, the god of music, had never heard anything like it. He stood still. He forgot the cows. This is worth fifty cows, he said. Where did you learn this?

So Hermes gave him the lyre. And Apollo gave Hermes the cattle, and a shining whip to herd them with, and his friendship for ever. From that day, the lyre belonged to Apollo, and Hermes became the messenger of the gods, the quick one, the guide of travellers, the friend of merchants and of thieves, the god who can slip through any door.

When the Greeks looked for a planet to give him, the choice was easy. Mercury is the fastest of all the wanderers. It runs around the Sun in just 88 days. It darts out into the twilight for a few evenings, then slips back into the Sun’s glare, then pops up again on the other side of the dawn, before you have noticed it was gone. It never strays far from the Sun, the way Hermes never strayed far from the gods he served. It is very hard to catch. Many people live their whole lives without seeing it.

So if you ever spot Mercury, low in the glow of the sunset, a small steady light that was not there last week, smile at it. You have caught the quickest god in the sky, and he will be gone again before you can tell anyone.

Find it tonight

Mercury never strays far from the Sun, so you can only catch it low in the twilight, for a week or two at a time: in the west just after sunset, or in the east just before dawn. Look for a small, steady, pinkish-white point close to the horizon. The Wanderers strip on the atlas page says whether this is one of its weeks.

Who tells this story

The ancient Greeks. The story is told in the Homeric Hymn to Hermes, a long, playful poem from around the 6th century BC (not by Homer, despite the name). The Greeks first called the planet Stilbon, “the gleaming one”, and later named it after Hermes, as the Babylonians had linked it with Nabû, the clever god of scribes; the Romans called him Mercury.
