A tale of The Persians, in the poetry of Nizami of Ganja · The Seven Wanderers

The King of the Seven Domes

Haft akhtar, the seven stars: Saturn, the Sun, the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter and Venus

Persian The palaces of the Sasanian king Bahram Gur, in the poem; written in Ganja (in today’s Azerbaijan) · 6 min read

Bahram Gur was the king of Persia, and he could do everything. He could shoot a wild ass at full gallop. He could play music and write poems. He was so famous for hunting the wild ass, the gur, that it became part of his name.

When he was a boy, he was sent to be raised at the court of an Arab king, in a palace called Khavarnaq, a palace so beautiful that people said it changed colour three times a day. And one day, wandering through it, the young prince found a door that was always kept locked.

He opened it.

Inside, on the walls, were seven portraits of seven princesses, each lovelier than the last. One was the daughter of the king of India. One came from Turkestan, one from Khwarazm, one from the land of the Slavs, one from the Maghreb in the far west, one from Rūm, the Byzantine land, and one from Iran itself. Seven princesses, from the seven climes of the world. And in the middle of them, there was an eighth portrait. It was of Bahram himself.

Under the paintings was written that one day, this prince would marry all seven.

Bahram locked the door again and kept the secret. Years passed. He fought for his throne and won it, and became king. And one by one, as the painting had promised, he married the seven princesses.

Then he called his architect, a master named Shida, who knew both building and the stars. Shida explained to the king that the world has seven climes, and each clime is ruled by one of the seven planets, and each planet rules one day of the week, and each has its own colour. Build each princess a dome, he said, in the colour of her planet. Then the stars will bless you.

So Shida built seven domes, and each was a world of one colour.

On Saturday, the day of Saturn, the king put on black and went to the black dome, where the princess of India lived.

On Sunday, the day of the Sun, he wore yellow, and went to the yellow dome, which shone like gold.

On Monday, the day of the Moon, he went to the green dome.

On Tuesday, the day of Mars, he went to the red dome, and everything around him was red.

On Wednesday, the day of Mercury, to the turquoise dome, the colour of the sky.

On Thursday, the day of Jupiter, to the dome the colour of sandalwood.

And on Friday, the day of Venus, he dressed all in white, and went to the white dome of the princess of Iran.

In each dome, after dinner, as the night came down, the princess told him a story. And each story was the colour of its dome.

In the black dome, the princess of India told of a king who always dressed in black. Once, in a strange city, he was carried away to a garden of unimaginable happiness, ruled by a fairy queen, and he was given everything, but he could not be patient, and wanted more, and in a moment it was all gone. Ever after, he wore black, in mourning for what he had lost.

In the red dome, the Slavic princess told of a princess who lived in a fortress on a mountain, and set riddles for every man who wanted to marry her; whoever failed, lost his head. One prince, wise and patient, solved every riddle and won her. Centuries later, through French and Italian retellings, that story became Puccini’s opera Turandot.

In the turquoise dome, the princess of the Maghreb told of Mahan, a young man lured into the desert at night by demons wearing his friends’ faces. Each beautiful place they led him to turned to a nightmare at dawn, until a stranger dressed in green, Khizr, the guide of the lost, took his hand and brought him home.

And so the week went on, from black to white, from Saturn to Venus. The stories began in loss and longing and grew, day by day, lighter and wiser. In the white dome, at the end of the week, the princess of Iran told the gentlest tale of all, of a young man whose every attempt to meet his beloved in secret is interrupted by some comic accident, until at last he marries her properly, in the light, with nothing to hide. Many readers see the whole week as a journey of the soul: from the darkness of Saturn to the pure white of Venus.

Bahram grew older. He began to see that his seven domes, for all their beauty, were a kind of dream. In the poem, he gives the domes away, to be turned into temples, and goes back to ruling his people with justice.

And then, one day, he went hunting for the wild ass, the gur, as he had all his life. He followed one into a cave. His companions waited outside. He never came out.

Persian has a famous joke about it, a verse later attributed to Omar Khayyam. In Persian, gur means both the wild ass and the grave. Bahram, who spent his whole life catching the gur: see how the gur has caught Bahram.

The seven planets are still up there, though, keeping the week in order. Tonight is one of Bahram’s days. Which one? Find the planet that rules it, and wear its colour.

All seven of Bahram’s planets are in our sky. The Sun and the Moon need no finding; for Saturn, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter and Venus, see the Wanderers strip on the atlas page, which shows where each one is tonight.

Who tells this story

The Persians. Bahram Gur was a real Sasanian king of Iran (Bahram V, who reigned 420–438), already a legend for his hunting and his love of music by the time the poet Nizami wrote about him in 1197. In the poem, the seven domes follow the old scheme of the seven planets, each ruling one of the seven climes of the world and one day of the week. Nizami’s Haft Paykar is one of the masterpieces of Persian literature, and its tales travelled far: the Tuesday story of the princess with her riddles became, by way of French and Italian retellings, Puccini’s opera Turandot.

Haft Paykar (“Seven Portraits” or “Seven Beauties”), a long poem by Nizami Ganjavi, completed in 1197.
