A tale of The Chinese · Cassiopeia

The Charioteer Who Would Not Cheat

Wáng Liáng (王良), the charioteer, with Cè (策), his whip, and Gédào (閣道), the covered road

Chinese The state of Jin, in the Spring and Autumn period (6th–5th century BC) · 4 min read

In ancient China, a chariot was a team. One man drove the horses, one man shot the arrows, and they had to trust each other completely. A good archer with a bad driver hit nothing. A great driver could make an ordinary archer look like a hero.

The greatest driver of his time was a man named Wang Liang.

He served a powerful lord of the state of Jin, Zhao Jianzi. Wang Liang knew horses the way a musician knows an instrument. He could make four horses run as one, turn on a coin, and keep a chariot steady over rough ground at full speed. And above all, he knew the rules of the hunt, the proper way of driving that had been taught since ancient times: you drove the chariot straight and fair, and the archer had to be good enough to make his shot.

One day, Lord Zhao Jianzi asked Wang Liang to drive for his favourite archer, a young man named Xi.

They went out hunting together. All day long, Wang Liang drove properly, exactly as the rules said. And all day long, Xi shot and missed. When the sun went down, they came home with nothing at all.

Xi went to the lord in a temper. “That Wang Liang,” he said, “is the worst charioteer in the whole world.”

Someone told Wang Liang what the archer had said. Wang Liang did not argue. He only asked: “Let me drive for him again.”

The archer did not want to go, but at last he agreed. So the next morning they set out once more. And this time Wang Liang drove differently. He swerved the chariot. He cut across the birds’ path. He brought the archer right up beside his targets, breaking every rule of the fair hunt. Xi could hardly miss. Before the morning was over, they had shot ten birds.

Now the archer was delighted. He ran to the lord. “Wang Liang,” he cried, “is the best charioteer in the whole world!”

Zhao Jianzi was pleased. “Then he shall be your driver,” he said. “From now on, always.”

He sent word to Wang Liang. And Wang Liang said no.

“When I drove for him properly, by the rules,” he said, “he could not hit a single bird all day. When I drove for him dishonestly, he hit ten in a morning. The old songs say that a true driver keeps to the right way, and a true archer’s arrow still flies home. I am not used to driving for a man who can only win by cheating. Please, let me decline.”

He refused to be the driver of a dishonest man, even for his lord’s favour, even though it would have made him rich. Many years later the philosopher Mencius told this story to his students. “Even a charioteer,” he said, “is ashamed to work hand in hand with a bad archer.” How much more should a good person refuse to bend what is right in order to please the powerful?

The Chinese did not forget Wang Liang. When they mapped their sky, which they organised like an empire, with an emperor, palaces, officials, and roads, they gave the greatest charioteer of all a place among the stars, high in the north. Near him they set his team of heavenly horses, in the Milky Way. And right in his hand they placed one more star: Cè, his whip.

The great historian Sima Qian wrote that when Wang Liang cracks his whip over his horses, chariots and horsemen will fill the land: a warning that war is coming. Star-watchers kept a careful eye on that whip.

And here is something they could not have known. Today, astronomers know that the whip star, the bright star in the middle of Cassiopeia’s W, is one of the strangest stars in the sky. It spins so fast that it throws off rings of gas, and every now and then, without warning, it brightens or fades. The charioteer’s whip really does move.

Find the W high in the north tonight. Wang Liang is there, holding the reins of the sky, the driver who would not cheat. And in the middle of the W, his whip waits, never quite still.

Find it tonight

In November evenings, Cassiopeia’s W is high in the north, near the top of the sky. Wang Liang’s stars lie along its western side, and the whip, Cè, is the bright middle star of the W (Gamma Cassiopeiae). Next to them, a line of stars across the Milky Way makes the Gédào, the covered road the Emperor travels on.

Show me these stars

Who tells this story

The Chinese, whose sky was organised like an empire on Earth, with an emperor, palaces, officials, roads and markets among the stars. Wang Liang was a real and famous charioteer of the state of Jin; Mencius used his story to teach about honesty. His stars, his horses and his whip have been part of the Chinese sky for more than two thousand years.

From the Mencius (4th century BC); the stars of Wáng Liáng appear in Sima Qian’s Records of the Grand Historian (about 100 BC).
