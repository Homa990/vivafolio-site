A tale of The Arabs of the desert · Cassiopeia

The Hand Stained With Henna

al-Kaff al-Khaḍīb (الكفّ الخضيب), “the hand stained with henna”

Arab Arabia · 3 min read

There is a tradition in many parts of the world that is older than anyone can say. Before a wedding, before a great festival, women paint their hands with henna, the reddish-orange dye made from the leaves of a desert shrub. The patterns are delicate and beautiful, and the colour stays on the skin for days, a sign that something joyful is happening.

The Arabs of the desert saw a painted hand in the sky.

But to understand whose hand it was, you have to start somewhere else, with the little cluster of stars we call the Pleiades. The Arabs called it al-Thurayyā, a name that means something like “the little abundant ones”, and it was one of the most loved and important groups of stars in their sky. Its rising and setting told the seasons, the rains and the time to travel.

In their imagination, al-Thurayyā grew. The bright little cluster became the head of a great figure stretching across the heavens, and from that head reached two enormous arms.

One arm reached down into the south, into the stars we now call Cetus, and it was short and ended without fingers. They called it al-Kaff al-Jadhmāʾ, the cut-off hand.

The other arm was long and graceful. It stretched up out of the head, through the stars of Perseus, through the soft river of the Milky Way, and up into the north. And there, at the end of it, five bright stars spread out like fingers: the five stars we call the W of Cassiopeia.

That was the hand stained with henna, al-Kaff al-Khaḍīb. Its five fingers were open, reaching across the top of the sky. And if you look carefully at the stars of the W, you will see that one of them, the star called Schedar, glows with a warm orange tinge, like the colour of henna drying on the skin.

The old star-watchers did not leave us a long myth about the hand. They did not explain why the figure’s hand was painted, or whose celebration it was. They left us something simpler: a picture. A great figure lying across the autumn sky, one hand broken and one hand beautiful, the beautiful one painted as if for a wedding.

The picture survived because of a Persian astronomer. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī was born in the city of Rayy, near today’s Tehran, in the year 903, and he wrote, as the scholars of his age did, in Arabic. Around 964 he finished his great Book of Fixed Stars. In it he did something generous: he kept both skies. He drew the Greek queen, whom he called Dhāt al-Kursī, “the lady of the chair”, sitting on her throne in these five stars, carefully drawn twice, once as you see her in the sky and once as she appears on a globe. And beside her he wrote down the old desert names, the ones the herders and travellers had always used: the great figure of al-Thurayyā, her two arms, and the henna-stained hand. Two pictures, two ways of seeing, the same five stars. Neither had to disappear for the other to be true.

Some of it survives in the names astronomers still use. The star at one end of the W is called Caph, from the Arabic al-kaff, “the palm of the hand”. Every time an astronomer anywhere in the world says that name, they are pointing, without knowing it, at the palm of a henna-painted hand.

On an autumn night, you can trace the whole figure yourself. Find the Pleiades rising in the east. Then follow the arm up through Perseus and the Milky Way to the W high in the north. Open your own hand and hold it up against those five stars. Something joyful is happening in the sky, and has been for thousands of years.

Find it tonight

In November evenings Cassiopeia’s W is high in the north, almost overhead. The Pleiades, the head of the great figure, rise in the east as night falls. Between them, through the Milky Way, runs the long arm of stars that joins them.

Who tells this story

The Arabs of the desert, who had their own pictures in the sky long before Greek astronomy reached them. This is less a story than a picture, and the old sources do not tell a full myth about it: what they record is the giant figure of al-Thurayyā, her two hands, and the names that survive. Later the Persian astronomer al-Ṣūfī drew the Greek queen in these stars too, dressed in the clothes of his own world, and kept the old desert names beside her.

Pre-Islamic Arab star lore, recorded by later scholars; the star name Caph (β Cassiopeiae) still carries it.
