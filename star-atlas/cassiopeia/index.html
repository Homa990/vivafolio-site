One sky, many eyes

Cassiopeia

Five bright stars in the shape of a W, high in the north, on the other side of the North Star from the Big Dipper. As the night turns, the W becomes an M, and later it stands on its end, so the stories about it often have someone sitting, falling or turning upside down. It lies right in the Milky Way, among some of the richest star fields of the northern sky, and it is part of a whole family of autumn constellations that all belong to one Greek tale.

One sky, many eyes: its other names

  • Kassiépeia (Κασσιέπεια) Ancient Greek: “the boastful queen on her throne”
  • al-Kaff al-Khaḍīb (الكفّ الخضيب) Arabic: “the hand stained with henna”
  • Dhāt al-Kursī (ذات الكرسي) al-Ṣūfī, the Persian astronomer (in Arabic): “the lady of the chair”
  • Wáng Liáng (王良) Chinese: “the great charioteer, with his whip and his horses”
  • Gédào (閣道) Chinese: “the covered road, a palace walkway across the sky”
  • Jungfru Marie stol Swedish folk name: “the Virgin Mary’s chair”
  • The W, or the M Stargazers everywhere: “for the shape, which turns as the night goes on”

Find it tonight

In autumn and early winter, Cassiopeia’s W is high in the north in the evening, almost overhead in November. It sits on the opposite side of the North Star from the Big Dipper, so when one is low, the other is high. From northern Europe and Canada it never sets.

What the telescope says

  • In 1572 a brand-new star blazed up in Cassiopeia, as bright as Venus. The young Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe measured it and proved that the heavens can change. It was an exploding star, a supernova.
  • Gamma Cassiopeiae, the middle star of the W, spins so fast it flings off a disc of gas, and brightens and fades without warning.
  • Cassiopeia lies in the Milky Way: through binoculars, the space around the W is full of faint stars and star clusters.
