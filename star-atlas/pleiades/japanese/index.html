The Lady Who Made a List of Stars

Subaru (昴), “gathered together into one”; also Mutsuraboshi, “the six linked stars”

Japanese The imperial court of Heian-kyō (Kyoto), and the farms and fishing villages of Japan · 3 min read

Around the year 1000, in the imperial city of Kyoto, a lady of the court sat down with a brush and a stack of fine paper, and began to make lists.

Her name, or the name she is remembered by, was Sei Shōnagon. She served the Empress, and she was clever, sharp-tongued and impossible to impress. She made lists of everything. Things that make the heart beat faster. Things that are hateful. Things that have lost their power. Things that are near yet far. Things that should be short. The lists, together with her stories and opinions, became one of the most famous books in Japanese literature: The Pillow Book.

And one day she wrote a list of stars.

It is a very short list. In Japanese, it begins: Hoshi wa subaru. “Stars: Subaru.”

That is all. No story, no explanation. She did not write that Subaru was a goddess or a hero. She simply put it first, the way you might put first, on a list of your favourite things, the one you love best. Then she named a few others, the herdsman star, the evening star, and moved on to something else.

Think of what that means. Of all the stars in the sky, including the brightest ones, this sharp, fashionable lady chose to name first a tiny, misty knot of faint stars that you can hardly count. Perhaps that was exactly why. Subaru is not dazzling. It is delicate. You have to look for it, and look again, and when your eyes adjust, more stars appear. It rewards patience. It is the kind of beauty the Heian court admired most.

The name itself is very old. Subaru comes from an old Japanese word that means to gather together, to unite into one: like beads threaded on a single string. Not seven separate stars, but one family of light.

But Subaru did not belong only to poets. Out in the countryside, far from the court, people who had never read The Pillow Book watched the same little cluster, and they gave it names of their own, taken from the things they used every day.

Farmers looked at it and saw a sheaf of grain, bound together at harvest, or a handful of seeds ready for planting. In fishing villages along the coast, people saw in it a piece of their fishing gear. Some called it Mutsuraboshi, the six linked stars. In the far south, in Okinawa, it was Murikabushi. Folklorists who travelled Japan in the twentieth century collected dozens of these names, from village after village, each one a little window into how people lived. One researcher remembered that her own grandmother, in Kōchi, had a name for it that meant the dab of a paint brush.

The same stars told the farmers and the fishermen when to work. When Subaru rose at a certain hour, it was time for a certain task: to sow, or to set out the nets. A court lady wrote it into literature, and a farmer wrote it into his fields.

There is a modern ending to this story too. In 1999, Japan finished building one of the largest telescopes in the world, on the summit of a volcano in Hawaiʻi, and they gave it this ancient name: Subaru. Far from Kyoto, an enormous mirror now gathers starlight together, as the old word says.

So Subaru has no dramatic tale of escape or punishment. What it has is a thousand years of people looking up and finding it beautiful, a court lady and a fisherman, a grandmother and an astronomer, each one seeing in it the thing they loved.

Find it tonight. Look straight at it, then just to one side, and watch the stars gather. Then ask yourself Sei Shōnagon’s question: of all the stars, which would you put first on your list?

Find it tonight

In November, Subaru rises in the east as darkness falls and is high in the south around midnight. Follow Orion’s belt up and to the right, past the orange star Aldebaran. In Japan it is a sign of autumn and winter nights.

Who tells this story

The Japanese, in literature and in country speech. This is not a myth with a plot: no old tale explains how Subaru came to be. What survives instead is something rarer, a thousand years of people noticing it and naming it after the things they held in their hands. The folklorist Takeshi Uchida recorded dozens of local names for the cluster across Japan.

Written down by Sei Shōnagon in The Pillow Book, around the year 1000; regional names gathered by Japanese folklorists in the 20th century.
