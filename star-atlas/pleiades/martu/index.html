A tale of The Martu of the Western Desert · The Pleiades

The Sisters With the Digging Sticks

Minyipuru, the Seven Sisters

Martu Martu Country, Western Australia, along what is now the Canning Stock Route · 3 min read

The desert of Western Australia is not empty. To the Martu, who have lived there for longer than anyone can count, every rock hole, every sandhill and every soak where water hides under the sand has a name and a story. A journey across it is not a crossing of nowhere. It is a walk through a living map, and the stories are the paths.

One of the oldest of those paths belongs to a group of women. The Martu call them the Minyipuru.

They set out from the far west, from the coast at a place now called Roebourne. At first there were many of them, mothers and sisters walking together into the desert. But the journey was long, and at place after place along the way, some of the women stayed behind, until only seven sisters were walking on.

They were not alone. Behind them, all the way from the coast, a man was following: an old man named Yurla, who wanted them. He kept to their tracks, mile after mile, day after day.

The sisters walked on through Martu Country. And at a place called Kalypa, a waterhole known today as Well 23 on the Canning Stock Route, they came upon a group of men.

The men saw seven women walking alone, and they thought these women would be easy to catch. They rushed at them and tried to grab them.

They were wrong.

Every desert woman carries a digging stick, a long, strong staff of hard wood, sharpened at one end. It is the tool she uses every day to dig for yams and honey ants and water. It is also a weapon, if it needs to be. The seven sisters turned around, raised their digging sticks, and fought back. They did not run. They chased the men, and beat them with their digging sticks, and left them lying on the ground. Then they picked up their things and walked on.

But Yurla was still following.

At a place called Pangkapini, he finally caught up with them, and he grabbed one of the sisters and would not let her go.

The other sisters did not abandon her. They were seven, and they meant to stay seven. They thought, and they made a plan, and they tricked the old man. And while he was fooled, they took their sister back.

Then the Minyipuru rose up from the earth and flew away to the east, across the whole of the desert, on their long journey across Australia, far out of Yurla’s reach. And at the end of it they became the stars: the little cluster of seven that shines in the desert sky.

Yurla never gave up. In many tellings across the desert, he is up there too, still following them across the sky, still never able to catch them.

Martu women have painted this story many times, in great canvases full of waterholes and journey lines, painted together, side by side, the way the sisters walked. When Martu women began to paint their stories for the wider world, Minyipuru was one of the first they chose to share.

So look up tonight and find the seven little stars. They are not seven helpless girls waiting to be saved. They are seven women who walked across a desert, fought off the men who tried to take them, and rescued one of their own. They are still together. They are still walking.

Find it tonight

In November the Pleiades rise in the east after dark and are high around midnight; from Australia they climb in the north-east, upside down to a northern eye. In the Western Desert the sisters’ seasons are tied to the cluster: they disappear into the Sun’s glow in May and return before dawn in June.

Who tells this story

The Martu, whose Country lies in the Western Desert of Western Australia. The Seven Sisters’ journey crosses the lands of many peoples, including the Ngaanyatjarra, Pitjantjatjara and Yankunytjatjara, and each tells its own part, with its own places and events. This retelling follows only the Martu part, as Martu women have shared it publicly through their paintings and the National Museum of Australia.

Minyipuru Jukurrpa, a Martu Dreaming shared publicly by Martu women artists of Martumili Artists, and through the National Museum of Australia’s exhibitions.
