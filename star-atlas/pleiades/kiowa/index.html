A tale of The Kiowa · The Pleiades

The Rock That Grew Into the Sky

Seven little stars in a group, standing over Tsoai in the winter night

Kiowa The northern plains, at Tsoai (Devils Tower), Wyoming · 3 min read

In the pine hills of the northern plains, a great column of grey stone rises straight out of the earth. It stands alone, taller than any tree, and it does not look like a mountain at all. Its sides are made of long, deep grooves running from the top to the bottom, as if something enormous had dragged its claws down the stone.

Something did. The Kiowa remember what.

They call the rock Tsoai, “Rock Tree”, and the story begins long ago, when the Kiowa were still living in the north, before they travelled south to the wide plains where they live today.

One day, seven little girls left their camp to play. They wandered as children do, farther than they should, laughing, chasing one another, until the tipis were far behind them. Then, suddenly, they saw the bears.

The bears had seen them first, and they were already coming.

The girls ran. They ran as fast as small legs can run, across the grass, between the trees, but bears are fast, and the bears were gaining on them. There was nowhere to hide. There was no one to help. Ahead of them there was only a low rock, sitting by itself in the grass, no taller than a child.

The girls scrambled up onto it, all seven, holding on to one another. And they prayed. They spoke to the rock itself, the way you might speak to a grandfather: Rock, have pity on us. Rock, save us.

The rock heard them.

It began to move. Slowly at first, then faster, it pushed itself up out of the ground, carrying the seven girls on its top. It grew taller than a man. It grew taller than the trees.

The bears reached the rock and leapt at it. They jumped as high as they could, and they clawed at its sides, trying to pull themselves up. Their claws tore long grooves into the stone. They slipped, fell back, and jumped again, and again their claws scraped down the rock, and still it kept rising.

Higher and higher it grew, until the girls at the top were only tiny shapes against the clouds, and the bears below could never reach them. And still the rock did not stop. It rose until the girls were pushed right up into the sky itself.

They are there now: seven little stars, close together in a group, the stars the world calls the Pleiades.

Afterwards, the people came to see the rock that had grown out of the earth. They walked all around its foot, and there, scattered on the ground, they found the bears’ claws, broken off in the struggle and turned to stone.

The rock is still there too, with the long scratch marks down every side, just as the story says. And on winter nights, if you stand near Tsoai in the middle of the night and look up, the seven little stars stand right above it, high over the rock that carried them home.

Find it tonight

In the middle of a winter night, the Pleiades stand high in the south. In November, look east after dark: past the orange star Aldebaran, a small silver knot of stars is climbing. That is where the seven girls went.

Who tells this story

The Kiowa, who lived in the northern plains before moving south to the plains of today’s Oklahoma. This telling was recorded in 1897 and is shared by the National Park Service at Devils Tower. Kiowa tellings differ: in N. Scott Momaday’s The Way to Rainy Mountain, the bear is the girls’ own brother and the sisters become the stars of the Big Dipper. The Lakota, Cheyenne, Crow, Arapaho and other nations tell their own stories of the same rock.

Told by a Kiowa soldier at Fort Sill in 1897 and kept by the National Park Service; told in Kiowa families for much longer.

With respect: Tsoai, which the Lakota call Mato Tipila (Bear Lodge), is a sacred place for many Plains nations. Prayer bundles are still left among its trees, and climbers are asked not to climb it in June, the month of many ceremonies.
