A tale of The ancient Greeks · The Pleiades

Seven Years of Running

Pleiades (Πλειάδες): “the daughters of Pleione”, a name that sounds like peleiades, “doves”

Greek Greece and the Aegean · 4 min read

Long before anyone in Greece owned a calendar, every farmer could read one. It hung in the sky. When a small, misty knot of stars climbed out of the dawn at the start of summer, it was time to bring in the harvest. When the same stars sank into the west at dawn, late in autumn, it was time to yoke the oxen and plough. Mothers taught it to children, and children to their children: watch the Seven.

But the Seven had not always been stars. Once, they were girls, and they were running.

Their father was Atlas, a Titan, one of the old giants who ruled the world before Zeus and the Olympian gods. When the gods won the long war against the Titans, Zeus gave Atlas the cruellest work of all. He was sent to the far western edge of the world, where the land ends and the evening begins, and there he had to hold up the sky on his shoulders. Forever.

Their mother was Pleione, a nymph of the ocean, and she raised their seven daughters among the green hills and meadows of Greece. Their names were Maia, the eldest and the shyest, then Electra, Taygete, Alcyone, Celaeno, Sterope, and Merope, the youngest. They walked the meadows together, a little flock of girls, always side by side.

One day, a hunter saw them.

His name was Orion, and he was a giant too: the tallest and strongest hunter who ever lived, with a club in his fist and a lion’s skin over his shoulder. He could stride through the sea with his head above the waves. When he saw Pleione and her seven daughters walking through the fields of Boeotia, he wanted them, and he began to follow.

They ran, and he ran after them.

They hid in forests, and he found them. They crossed rivers, and he waded through. They climbed into the mountains, and his long legs brought him up behind them. Days became seasons, and seasons became years. For seven years, the story says, the sisters ran across Greece with the hunter’s footsteps always somewhere behind them.

You might ask: where was their father? Atlas could see everything from the edge of the world. He could see his daughters stumbling through the hills. But he could not let go of the sky. If he moved his hands, it would fall on all of them. So he stood, and he held it, and he watched.

At last, worn out, the sisters stopped running and called out to the king of the gods. “Zeus,” they cried, “we cannot run any longer. Hide us where he can never reach.”

Zeus heard them. In the space of a breath, the seven girls became seven white doves. Their arms became wings, and they rose out of Orion’s reach, higher and higher, past the treetops and the clouds, until they flew all the way into the sky. There Zeus fixed them among the stars, close together, as they had always walked. The Greeks called them the Pleiades, and the name sounds almost exactly like their word for doves.

Some said the doves were given a duty too. Even in Homer’s day, people told of doves that carry ambrosia, the food of the gods, across the sky to Zeus. And here is a small comfort the sisters had in their new home: from the sky, at last, they could see their father, and he could see them, every night.

But look closely and you will notice something strange. There were seven sisters, yet most people see only six stars. Where is the seventh?

The Greeks had two answers. Some said it was Merope. All her sisters had married gods, but Merope fell in love with a mortal man, the clever king Sisyphus, and in the sky she was ashamed to shine as brightly as the others, so she hides her face. Others said it was Electra. Her son was the ancestor of the kings of Troy, and when that great city burned, she covered her eyes so that she would not have to watch. Either way, one sister is still there, dim and quiet, if your eyes are very sharp.

And Orion? He did not escape the story either. When he died, after a life of hunting, the gods placed him in the sky as well: the giant with the three bright stars of his belt.

So the chase never ended. It simply moved upstairs.

Go out on a winter evening and watch. First, low in the east, the little silver cluster of the Seven Sisters climbs into the dark. Then, an hour or two later, a belt of three bright stars rises after them: Orion, still following. All night they cross the sky in the same order, the doves ahead, the hunter behind. He has been running for three thousand years. He has never come one step closer.

Find it tonight

In November the little cluster rises in the east as night falls and stands high in the south around midnight. Find Orion’s belt, follow it up and to the right past the orange star Aldebaran, and the next misty knot of stars is the Seven Sisters. An hour or two behind them, Orion climbs over the horizon.

Who tells this story

The ancient Greeks, from Hesiod’s farming poem Works and Days to the Roman writers who collected the old star myths (Hyginus, Ovid). Versions differ: some say Orion chased the sisters with their mother for five years, most say seven; some say Merope is the hidden star, others Electra.

Told since at least the 8th century BC: Hesiod already names the Pleiades “fleeing mighty Orion”.

Where this story comes from

  1. Hesiod, Works and Days, lines 383–387 and 615–621 (the Pleiades as the farmer’s calendar, fleeing Orion)
  2. Hyginus, De Astronomia 2.21 (the seven-year chase, the dimmed star)
  3. Ovid, Fasti 4.169–178 (Merope and Electra, the star that hides)
  4. Homer, Odyssey 12.62–63 (the doves that carry ambrosia to Zeus)
  5. Theoi Project: the Pleiades, with the ancient passages in translation
