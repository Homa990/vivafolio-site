A tale of Quechua and Aymara farmers of the Andes, heirs of the Inca · The Pleiades

The Storehouse That Forecasts the Rain

Qullqa (also written Collca or Qollqa), “the storehouse”, in Quechua

Andean The high Andes of Peru and Bolivia · 4 min read

It is June in the high Andes, the middle of winter, and it is very cold. Two hours before sunrise, an old farmer wraps a poncho around her shoulders and climbs the hill above her village. Others are already there, standing in the dark, breath steaming, all of them facing the same way: north-east, where the mountains are black against a sky just beginning to pale.

They are waiting for the Storehouse.

In Quechua, the language of the Inca, the little cluster of stars we call the Pleiades is called Qullqa. The word means a storehouse: the stone granaries that the Inca built by the thousands along their mountain roads, filled with dried potatoes, maize and quinoa against a hungry year. A storehouse is a promise that the future has been thought about. And for the farmers on the hill, the stars that bear its name hold a promise too.

For about a month the Storehouse has been missing, lost in the glare of the Sun. Now, around the feast of San Juan, it comes back. It rises just before dawn, low over the mountains, for a few minutes before the sky grows too bright.

The farmers are not just watching it. They are judging it.

If the Storehouse rises big and bright, its stars sharp and many, it will be a good year. The rains will come on time. They can plant their potatoes at the usual time, in October and November, and expect a good harvest.

But if the Storehouse looks small, dim and blurry, its stars hard to count, it is a warning. The rains will come late, and they will be poor. The wise thing is to wait, and to plant later, so that the young potatoes are not caught by drought.

Then the farmers walk back down the hill, and they decide.

For centuries, visitors to the Andes heard about this and smiled politely. How could seven little stars in June know anything about rain in December? It sounded like a beautiful superstition.

Then, in the year 2000, three scientists decided to check. An anthropologist, Benjamin Orlove, and two climate scientists, John Chiang and Mark Cane, collected the farmers’ observations and compared them with decades of weather records and satellite measurements of the sky.

The farmers were right.

Here is what the scientists found. In some years, very high in the atmosphere, there is a thin, almost invisible veil of cloud, so thin that you would never notice it in daylight. But it is just enough to dim the faint stars near the horizon at dawn, and make the Storehouse look smaller and paler. And those thin high clouds come most often in the years when the Pacific Ocean is warming up into El Niño, the great shift in the ocean that brings drought to the southern Andes many months later.

The dim stars in June were telling the farmers that El Niño was coming. The farmers had been reading the ocean, from the top of a mountain, by looking at the sky. And they had been doing it for centuries, long before anyone had heard of El Niño.

The Andean sky holds other wonders too. Across it flows Mayu, the river, the band of light we call the Milky Way, and in its dark patches the Andean peoples see animals made not of stars but of shadow: a toad, a snake, a partridge, and a mother llama with her baby, whose shining eyes are two bright stars. But the Storehouse is the one the farmers climb the hill for.

So the story of Qullqa has no hero and no monster. Its hero is a woman on a cold hill before dawn, looking carefully, and remembering what her parents taught her. And its ending is not in the sky at all. It is in a field, months later, where the potatoes were planted at exactly the right time.

If you look at the Pleiades tonight, look at them the Andean way. Not just is it beautiful? but how clear is it? how many stars can I count? The sky is always telling us something. The question is whether we have learned how to listen.

Find it tonight

In November the Pleiades rise in the east as darkness falls and stand high around midnight. To see them the Andean way, wait for June and look low in the north-east, about an hour before sunrise, when the little cluster first returns from behind the Sun.

Who tells this story

Quechua- and Aymara-speaking farming communities of the central Andes, whose knowledge goes back to Inca times and before. This is not an origin myth but a living practice, a way of reading the sky. Communities differ: some judge the cluster’s size, some its brightness, some the date it appears, and the name Qullqa is also given in some places to other star groups. In the Inca empire, qullqas were the great storehouses of food that lined the royal roads.

Watched for at least four hundred years: Spanish chroniclers recorded it soon after the conquest, and farmers still do it today. Studied by scientists in 2000.

With respect: This is the knowledge of living Andean communities, described here from published research and museum accounts. The farmers are the experts; the scientists came later.
