A tale of Māori of Aotearoa New Zealand · The Pleiades

The Eyes of the Wind

Matariki: from Ngā Mata o te Ariki Tāwhirimātea, “the eyes of the god Tāwhirimātea”

Māori Aotearoa New Zealand · 4 min read

In the deepest part of a New Zealand winter, in late June or early July, people climb hills in the dark and face the east. It is cold. The grass is wet. Everyone is waiting for the same thing: a small, faint cluster of stars that has been missing from the sky for a month, hidden in the glare of the Sun.

When at last it appears, low and trembling just above the horizon before dawn, the people call out the names of everyone they lost during the year. They cook food and offer its steam to the sky. And a new year begins.

The stars are Matariki. And they are the eyes of a god.

In the beginning, the sky and the earth were not apart. Ranginui, the Sky Father, lay in a tight embrace with Papatūānuku, the Earth Mother, and between them there was no room for light. Their many children lived in the dark, cramped space between their parents’ bodies, unable to stand up straight, never seeing the day.

The children grew tired of the darkness, and they talked about what to do. Tūmatauenga, the fiercest of them, said they should kill their parents. But Tāne-mahuta, the father of the forests, said no: “Let us push them apart. Let the sky stand far above us, and let the earth stay close beside us, as our mother.”

One by one, the brothers agreed. All except one. Tāwhirimātea, the god of the winds and the weather, would not agree. He loved his parents as they were, together. He would not help to tear them apart.

The brothers tried, one after another, to push their parents apart, and one after another they failed. Then Tāne lay down on his back, placed his feet against the sky, and pushed with all his strength, slowly, slowly, until the Sky Father was lifted high above the Earth Mother. Light poured into the world for the first time.

Ranginui and Papatūānuku wept. The sky’s tears still fall as rain, and the mist that rises from the earth is the mother’s sighing for her husband.

But Tāwhirimātea could not forgive his brothers. He rose up to live with his father in the sky, and from there he made war on them. He sent the winds, the clouds, the hurricanes. He tore down the great forests of Tāne. He whipped up the seas until his brother Tangaroa’s waters crashed against the land. Rongo and Haumia-tiketike, the gods of the planted food and the wild food, hid from the storm inside their mother, the earth. Only Tūmatauenga, the god of people, stood firm against him, and could not be beaten.

In the end, Tāwhirimātea’s anger and grief were so great that he did something terrible. He tore out his own eyes. He crushed them in his hands and threw them up into the heavens, where they stuck to the chest of his father, the sky.

And they are still there: a small, bright cluster of stars, the eyes of the god of the wind. Ngā Mata o te Ariki Tāwhirimātea. Matariki.

Over the generations, the people learned that the eyes had more than one story to tell. Many Māori astronomers, and Professor Rangi Mātāmua’s ancestors among them, counted nine stars in the cluster, and each one looks after part of the world.

Matariki herself, the brightest, watches over the health and wellbeing of people. Pōhutukawa gathers up those who have died during the year and carries them across the sky. Waitī cares for fresh water and everything that lives in it; Waitā for the ocean and its food. Waipuna-ā-rangi is the rain. Tupu-ā-nuku looks after everything that grows in the soil, and Tupu-ā-rangi everything that grows high up: fruit, berries and the birds of the forest. Ururangi is the star of the winds, the place that belonged to Tāwhirimātea all along. And Hiwa-i-te-rangi, the youngest and smallest, is the star of wishes. People send their hopes for the new year to her.

So when Matariki returns in midwinter, the people read it like a letter. If the stars rise clear and bright and twinkling, it will be a warm and plentiful year. If they look hazy and huddled together, the year will be cold, and they should plant with care. Then they speak to the stars: the names of the dead, for Pōhutukawa to carry; and their wishes, sent up to Hiwa.

Look up from Aotearoa before a winter dawn and you will see them, nine small lights close together, low in the north-east. A god who could not bear to see his parents torn apart gave away his sight. And because of it, every year, a whole people is given a way to see the year ahead.

Find it tonight

In the northern hemisphere, the cluster is high in the south around midnight in November. In Aotearoa it shines in the north on summer evenings, then disappears into the Sun’s glow in May. Around late June or early July it returns, low in the north-east just before dawn: that is Matariki rising, and the Māori New Year. To find it, Te Papa teaches a path: start from Tautoru, the three stars of Orion’s belt, follow them to Te Kokotā, the V-shaped group of stars beside them, and keep going: the little cluster beyond is Matariki.

Who tells this story

Māori, the Indigenous people of Aotearoa New Zealand. Iwi (tribes) hold their own versions: some count seven stars in Matariki, others nine, and some iwi, where Matariki is hard to see, mark the new year by the star Puanga (Rigel) instead. The meaning “little eyes” is also heard, but Professor Rangi Mātāmua notes that the old stories support “the eyes of the god”. Since 2022, Matariki has been a public holiday in New Zealand.

An ancestral Māori tradition, gathered and shared today by Māori astronomers such as Professor Rangi Mātāmua.

With respect: The names, tasks and stories of the Matariki stars belong to Māori. This retelling follows what Māori scholars and institutions have shared publicly; each iwi has its own knowledge.
