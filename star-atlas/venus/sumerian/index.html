A tale of The Sumerians of ancient Iraq · Venus

The Queen Who Went Down Into the Dark

Inanna, the Lady of Heaven, whose star is the planet Venus

Sumerian The cities of Sumer, between the Tigris and the Euphrates (southern Iraq) · 6 min read

In the beginning of evening, when the Sun has gone and the sky turns violet, one light appears before all the others, so bright it seems to have been lit by hand. The Sumerians, who lived four thousand years ago between two great rivers in what is now Iraq, knew who she was. She was Inanna, the Lady of Heaven, goddess of love and of war, and that light was her star.

She was the proudest of all the gods. She owned the sky. She owned cities and temples. And one day, the old poem says, from the great heaven she set her mind on the great below. She decided to go down to the underworld, the land from which no one returns, ruled by her older sister Ereshkigal.

Nobody knows exactly why. Perhaps she wanted that kingdom too. But Inanna was not a fool. Before she left, she called her faithful servant Ninshubur and gave her instructions. “If I do not come back in three days,” she said, “beat the drum for me. Go to the great gods. Ask them to save me.”

Then she dressed as a queen. She put a crown on her head, strings of lapis lazuli beads around her neck, twin beads on her breast, and a royal robe over her body. She painted her eyes. She put on a golden breastplate, a golden ring, and took in her hand a measuring rod and line of lapis lazuli, the tools of a ruler. Dressed in all her power, she walked to the gates of the underworld and knocked.

The gatekeeper, Neti, went to tell his queen. Ereshkigal listened, and slapped her thigh, and bit her lip. Then she said: Let the seven gates of the underworld be bolted. Then open each one a crack. And as she enters, take something from her.

So Inanna went down.

At the first gate, they took her crown. “What is this?” she cried. “Be silent, Inanna,” said the gatekeeper. “The ways of the underworld are perfect. You must not question them.”

At the second gate, they took the beads from her neck. At the third, the beads on her breast. At the fourth, the breastplate. At the fifth, the golden ring. At the sixth, the rod and line. At the seventh gate, they took the royal robe from her body.

Each time she asked, and each time the answer was the same: Be silent, Inanna. The ways of the underworld are perfect.

And so the Queen of Heaven came before her sister with nothing at all.

Ereshkigal rose from her throne. The seven judges of the underworld looked at Inanna with the look of death. They spoke against her with angry words. And Inanna was turned into a corpse, and the corpse was hung on a hook on the wall.

Up above, three days and three nights went by.

Ninshubur did not forget. She beat the drum and tore her clothes and went from god to god. She went to Enlil, the great god, in his city: he would not help. She wanted the underworld, he said. Whoever goes there stays there. She went to Nanna, the Moon god, in his city: he would not help either.

Then she went to Enki, the god of wisdom and sweet waters, and Enki was troubled. What has my daughter done? he said. I am worried. And he did something no other god would think of. From the dirt under his fingernails, he made two tiny creatures, so small and strange that no gatekeeper would notice them, and he gave one the food of life and the other the water of life.

The two little creatures slipped through the gates like flies. They found Ereshkigal lying in pain and groaning, and they did a simple thing: they felt sorry for her. When she groaned, Oh, my heart!, they groaned with her: You are troubled, our mistress: oh, your heart! When she groaned, Oh, my liver!, they groaned too: Oh, your liver! No one had ever shared her pain before. Grateful, she offered them a gift: a river full of water, a field full of grain. They refused. They asked only for the corpse hanging on the hook.

They sprinkled it with the food of life and the water of life. And Inanna rose.

But the underworld keeps its laws. No one leaves without sending someone else down in her place. So as Inanna climbed back up through the seven gates, demons came with her, demons who eat no food and drink no water and know no kindness, clinging to her side, waiting for her to choose.

At the top, Ninshubur was waiting in the dust, dressed in rags of mourning. “Take her,” said the demons. “Never!” said Inanna. “She saved my life.” They went on, and found others of her household weeping for her in mourning clothes. She would not give them up either.

Then they came to the great apple tree in the plain of Kulaba. And there sat Dumuzi, the shepherd, Inanna’s own husband, on a magnificent throne, in a splendid robe. He was not mourning at all.

Inanna looked at him. It was the look of death. Take him, she said.

Dumuzi wept. He lifted his hands to the Sun god and begged to be changed, and he ran, and hid, but the demons found him. In the end, mercy came from an unexpected place. His sister, Geshtinanna, loved him so much that she offered to share his fate. And so it was decided, in the last lines of the poem: You for half the year, and your sister for half the year. When one is in the underworld, the other is free.

The tablets end with praise, the way Sumerian poems do. Holy Ereshkigal, great is your renown! Holy Ereshkigal, I sing your praises! Even the dark queen gets her due.

Now look at Venus. For months she rules the evening, the brightest light in the west. Then, night by night, she sinks lower into the sunset, and one evening she is simply gone. For a while there is no Queen of Heaven at all. And then, one morning, there she is again in the east before dawn, brighter than ever, rising out of the darkness on the other side.

The tablets never say that this is why the story was told. But stand outside on the evening Venus first returns, and it is hard not to think of it: the queen who went down into the dark, gave up everything she had at seven gates, and came back.

Find it tonight

Venus is the brilliant white light low in the west after sunset, or low in the east before dawn, depending on the season. Every year and a half or so it slips into the Sun’s glare and is lost from sight, for a few days or for a couple of months, before it returns on the other side of the dawn.

Show me these stars

Who tells this story

The Sumerians of southern Mesopotamia, the first people known to have written. Inanna (later Ishtar in Akkadian) was the goddess of love and war and the Lady of Heaven, and her star was the planet Venus: hymns praise her as the shining star of evening and morning, and her symbol was an eight-pointed star. Whether the tale of her descent was first told about the planet’s disappearances is an idea some scholars have proposed, not something the tablets say. This retelling follows the Sumerian poem known as Inana’s descent to the nether world, as translated by the Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature (University of Oxford).

Written on clay tablets in the Sumerian language, in copies of about 1900–1600 BC; the story is older.
