A tale of Persian scholars, painters and poets · Venus

The Woman Who Climbed Into the Sky

al-Zuhara in Arabic, Zohreh or Nāhīd in Persian: the planet Venus

Persian Babylon, in the tale; written down and sung in Iran · 5 min read

Long ago, the story goes, the angels looked down from heaven at the people of the Earth, and they were not impressed.

Every day they watched humans lie, steal, quarrel, drink, and break every promise they had made. And the angels said to God: How can they? They have everything. How can they be so weak?

And God answered: If you had what they have inside them, their hungers and their desires, you would do no better.

The angels did not believe it. So, the story says, they chose two of the best and wisest among them, Hārūt and Mārūt, and the two were given human hearts, with every human longing in them, and sent down to the Earth, to the great city of Babylon. There they would be judges. They were forbidden four things only: to worship anything but God, to take what was not theirs to take, to kill, and to drink wine. Every evening, when the day’s judging was done, they spoke a secret word, the great Name of God, and rose back up into heaven, and every morning they came down again.

For a time, all went well. They were fair, and patient, and the people of Babylon came to them from far away.

Then one day a woman came to their court.

She had a quarrel with her husband, and she wanted justice. But neither of the two judges heard a word of her case. She was the most beautiful woman they had ever seen. The Arabs call her Zuhara, and the Persians Zohreh, or Nāhīd: the radiant one.

From that day on, Hārūt and Mārūt could think of nothing else. They begged her to love them. She laughed at them. You are judges, she said. Prove that you love me. Bow down to my idol. They refused. Then kill for me. They refused again. Then at least, she said, drink a cup of wine with me.

Wine, they thought, was the smallest of the four. So they drank.

And as the wine went to their heads, the other three fell one by one. In some versions they bowed to her idol; in some they killed a man who had seen them. The tellers do not agree on everything. They all agree on what came next.

Zohreh said: I have one more wish. Teach me the word you say every evening, the word that carries you up into heaven.

And, foolish with wine and with love, they taught it to her.

She spoke the word. And she rose. Up from the room, up from the city, up past the clouds, into the evening sky. The angels watched her go, and found they could not follow. They said the word, and nothing happened. The word would no longer lift them. Up there, God turned the woman into a star: the brightest star of all, the one that shines alone in the west after sunset. Venus.

Hārūt and Mārūt had lost their way home. They were offered a choice: to be punished now, in this world, or later, in the next. They chose this world, because it would end. And so, the story says, they hang to this day in a deep well in Babylon, upside down, between earth and sky, waiting for the end of time. In some tellings, people would climb down to them to learn secrets, and the angels always warned them first: We are only a trial. Do not lose your faith.

That is the old tale, and the scholars argued about it for a thousand years. Many said that angels could never sin, and that this was a borrowed legend, and they were probably right about where it came from.

But the poets did not care. In Persian poetry, Zohreh became something else entirely: not a temptress, but the musician of heaven. She sits in the third sky with her harp, and her music is so beautiful that the planets dance. Hafez, the great poet of Shiraz, boasts about his own verses with her: No wonder if, up in heaven, at the words of Hafez, the song of Zohreh sets the Messiah dancing.

Tonight, look west after sunset, at the lamp that comes out before every other light. Two angels lost the sky for that star. Then a poet gave her back her harp.

Find it tonight

Zohreh is the brightest light of the evening or the morning sky, depending on the season: a steady white lamp low in the west after sunset, or low in the east before dawn. She never climbs high, and she never strays far from the Sun.

Who tells this story

This is a story told around a single verse of the Qur’an (2:102), which says only that two angels in Babylon, Hārūt and Mārūt, taught people a dangerous knowledge, always warning them first: “We are only a trial, so do not lose your faith.” Everything else, the angels’ boast, the woman and her ascent, comes from early commentaries and storytellers, and it was a Persian, al-Ṭabarī of Āmol in Tabaristan, who collected the versions, which differ in their details. Many later Muslim scholars rejected the tale as unreliable, saying angels cannot sin, and it has close cousins in older Jewish legends. Persian poets loved it anyway, and Zohreh, the Nāhīd of old Iran, became their musician of the sky.

Written down around AD 900 by the Persian scholar al-Ṭabarī of Āmol, in his commentary on the Qur’an (in Arabic, the scholars’ language of his day); painted in al-Qazwīnī’s Wonders of Creation; sung by Persian poets such as Hafez for centuries after.

With respect: The Qur’anic verse is part of a living scripture. This page retells the tale that grew around it as folklore and poetry, not as religious teaching.
