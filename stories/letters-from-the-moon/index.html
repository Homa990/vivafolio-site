Tales for After Dark · A Correspondence

Letters from the Moon

A curious correspondence from beyond our skies.

By Orion Belle · Keeper of Undeliverable Letters, the Night Post House

8-minute read by candlelight

Everyone looks at the moon. Almost no one thinks to write.

The first letter came on a Tuesday, which is not a day on which anything remarkable is supposed to happen. It was lying on the sorting table in the Night Post House when I unlocked the door, although the door had been locked all night and the windows painted shut since before the war. The envelope was cold. Not chilly, the way paper is in November, but cold the way a stone is cold at the bottom of a well, and when I turned it over it left a faint silver dust on my fingers that would not wash off for three days.

It was addressed, in a hand so fine it looked engraved, to The One Who Paints Me, c/o the Night Post House, Wick Street.

I assumed, as you would, that it was a joke. The owls who fly our night rounds are a mischievous lot, and so are the boys who feed them. But nobody owned up, and when I held the paper to the lamp I saw that it was not paper at all. It was thinner than onion skin and it shone faintly, the way frost shines before anyone has walked on it.

The lamp attic of the Post House has not been used since 1911. I went up with a candle. Under a sheet, under a century of dust, there was an easel, a chair and a stack of small canvases tied with a faded ribbon. Every one of them was the moon. Hundreds of moons: a crescent like a fingernail, a full moon swollen and orange over the rooftops, a moon half-hidden behind a chimney as if it were shy. The last canvas was only half painted. In the corner of each one, in tiny gold letters, was the name M. Vell.

The girl in the attic

Marguerite Vell was the last lamp-girl the Post House ever had. The ledgers say she was hired at fourteen to keep the attic lamp burning, so that the night owls could find their way home with the post, and that she never once went home before midnight. They say she was quiet and plain and talked to no one. And they say that on the night of the winter solstice, 1911, she climbed the stairs to the attic and was never seen again. Her coat was still on its hook in the morning. The window was open. There was frost on the sill in the shape of a footprint, a small one, pointing out.

I wrote back. I did not know what else to do. I wrote that Marguerite was gone, that she had been gone for more than a hundred years, and that I was very sorry. I left the letter on the attic sill under a saucer of milk, which my grandmother always said was the polite thing to leave for anything that comes in the night. In the morning the milk was gone and the saucer was full of frost.

Everyone looks at me, and no one ever looks back.

I could not bring myself to answer that one. For four nights I sat in the attic with the half-painted canvas, and I am ashamed to say I wept over it, because I understood something then that I had not understood before. The moon had been counting on Marguerite. Not on her paintings, on her. On the small square of yellow light in a window, on the one face out of all the millions in the world that turned upward on purpose.

On the fifth night the owl came. A barn owl, white as a plate, with a letter held in its beak, and it landed on the easel and fixed me with its black eyes and would not move. I understood that I was meant to read it aloud. So I did, with my voice shaking, to an owl, in an attic, at three in the morning.

The lamp at the edge

There was no moon that night. It was the new moon, the dark of the month, when the sky is at its blackest. I stood on the roof of the Post House with a pair of brass opera glasses borrowed from the clockmaker next door, feeling like a fool. And then, low over the river, where the moon should have been and was not, I saw it. A single pinprick of warm, yellow light. Not a star. Stars are white and cold and they flicker. This was steady, and golden, and small, like a candle in an attic window seen from very far away.

I am not a sentimental man. I have sorted the undeliverable letters of this city for thirty-one years: letters to the drowned, to the wind, to people who moved away without a forwarding address. But I lifted my hand, there on the roof, and I waved. And the light, I swear it on my mother, the light went out and came back on again. Once. Twice. The way you flick a lamp to say goodnight to someone in the street.

The last letter came this morning. I have not yet decided whether to believe it, so I am printing it, and you may decide for me.

The letter was dated tomorrow. I have checked the calendar four times. Tonight, after I lock up, I am going to take that half-painted canvas home with me and set it up by my window. I do not know how to paint. I suspect that does not matter in the slightest.

The blank sheet

When I lifted the canvas from the easel, a single sheet of paper slid out from behind it and floated, quite slowly, to the floor. It was blank. Or it seemed blank, until the owl leaned down from the rafters and brushed it with the tip of one white wing, and for a moment, where the feather passed, I saw silver writing come and go. Moonlight ink. It only shows for those who wave something over it. I am printing it exactly as I found it. You will have to wave yourself.

The solstice, 1911

To whoever finds the lamp: do not be afraid of the dark part of the moon. That is where the kindest light is kept. I have left you my brushes, my chair, and the half of her I never finished. Paint it however you feel; she always liked that best. And on clear nights, if you see a small gold light at her edge, do not wait for it to wave. I will be waving first. Marguerite
