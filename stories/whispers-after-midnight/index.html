The Night the Portraits Stepped Out

What the night reveals when the crowds are gone.

By Jasper Cale · Apprentice Night-Watchman, the Galerie des Vernis

10-minute read by candlelight

Some things can only be seen once. That is why they must be seen.

The rule of the Galerie des Vernis is very simple, and the old night guard told it to me as he handed over his keys: you may stay all night, but you must not fall asleep, and whatever you see, you must not let the paintings out of your sight. I thought it was the sort of thing old night guards say to frighten new apprentices. He did not smile when he said it. He shook my hand for a long time, and then he went home, and I was alone with four hundred portraits and the sound of my own heart.

At midnight the clock in the east hall struck, and on the twelfth stroke somebody sneezed.

It was Madame Vernisse, the red-haired lady in the purple robe embroidered with stars, who hangs in the second room and who, it turns out, has opinions about everybody. “Dust,” she said, quite crossly, “nearly a hundred and forty years of it. Does nobody clean the frames any more?” And then, because I was staring at her with my mouth open: “Oh, close your mouth, young man, you look like a carp. Sir Viridian, wake up. We have a visitor, and he is wearing brown shoes with a black coat.”

The Turning

You may have heard that the portraits of the Galerie move when no one is looking. What you will not have heard, because the portraits made me promise not to tell it until now, is that one night every year, on the longest night, they may step out of their frames for a single hour. But only if a living person keeps watch, and does not sleep, and counts them all back in before the bell.

That night, I was the living person.

I do not have the words for that hour, though I have tried for weeks to find them. Dame Lazuli in her blue velvet came down out of her frame like someone stepping off a carriage, and Maestro Carmine bowed to her, and they danced in the empty hall to music only they could hear, her lace sweeping the floor, his old knees creaking. A Dutch merchant ate the painted grapes from a still life two rooms over and laughed until he cried. A general who had been painted on horseback for two centuries got down and simply lay on the cool marble floor with his arms spread out, saying, “Oh, my back. Oh, my back.”

They danced in the empty hall to music only they could hear.

And in the smallest room, in the smallest frame, there was a girl.

She was perhaps seven, painted in dark oils, in a nightgown, holding a candle, and she was the only one who did not come out. She just stood at the edge of her frame looking at the window, at the black square of night, as if she were waiting for something. The brass label beneath her said only: Wren. Painted by her father, 1689.

“Leave her be,” Dame Lazuli whispered to me as she waltzed past. “She has never seen anything but candles. Her father painted her after the fever took her, from memory, by candlelight. She has never seen a morning. None of us have, in here. The gallery has no windows facing east.”

The empty frame

When the clock struck one, the portraits went back. All of them, one after another, grumbling, yawning, the general complaining about his saddle, and I counted them in. Three hundred and ninety-nine. I counted again. Three hundred and ninety-nine.

The smallest frame was empty. There was only the dark painted wall, and a single drop of wax on the painted floor.

Sir Viridian, who is a critic and has never been kind about anything, went quite white beneath his varnish. “If a painting is not back in its frame by the first light of the sun,” he said, “it is neither painted nor living. It is simply gone. Every colour of her. Run, boy.”

I ran. The back door was open, and there were small wet footprints on the cobbles, made of oil paint, a dark umber brown. I followed them through the sleeping city, down the Rue des Vernis, past the shuttered cafés and the cathedral and the bridge, and they did not go anywhere you would expect a lost child to go. They went east. Always east. Uphill.

I found her at a quarter to five, on the top of the old city wall, sitting with her painted knees drawn up under her nightgown and her candle beside her, looking at the place where the sky was beginning, very faintly, to turn grey.

“You have to come back,” I said. I was out of breath. “Wren, please. The sun. You will disappear.”

She looked up at me with eyes that were only brushstrokes and were somehow, impossibly, the most alive eyes I have ever seen. “I know,” she said. “Father told me about mornings. He said they were the most beautiful thing in the world. He said I would see a hundred thousand of them. I have only ever seen candles.” She turned back to the east. “Just one,” she said. “Please. I would like to see just one.”

One sunrise

I was the watchman that night, and I am supposed to tell you what a watchman should have done. I should have picked her up and carried her back. I think I could have made it. Instead I sat down beside her on the cold stones, and I took off my coat and put it around her shoulders, and we watched.

It came up slowly, the way it always does, the way you and I have seen it come up so many times that we no longer bother to look. First the grey, then a thin line of rose along the rooftops, then gold, then a great spill of light across the river and the bridges and the wet slate roofs, until the whole city looked as if it had been painted that minute by someone who loved it very much.

Wren did not say anything. She only held my sleeve and watched, and as the light reached us she began to grow pale, then golden, then pale again, the colours lifting off her like mist off a pond. She was smiling. At the very end, when there was hardly anything left of her but the outline of a smile and the warmth of a small hand on my sleeve, she whispered: “Oh. Father was right.”

And then my coat was empty.

I walked back to the gallery in the full morning light, and I could not see the street for crying. I unlocked the smallest room to face the empty frame and tell the others I had failed them.

The frame was not empty.

Wren was in it. But she was no longer a child in the dark with a candle. She stood on a city wall in a coat several sizes too big for her, in a blaze of sunrise painted in colours I have never seen on any canvas: rose and saffron and a gold so bright it hurt to look at. The candle was gone. She did not need it any more. And when I put my hand very close to the canvas, not touching, I promise I did not touch, it was warm.

The new catalogue lists the painting as Wren at Dawn, artist unknown. The guards say it has always hung there. If you visit, you must not touch it. But if you stand very still in the smallest room, late in the afternoon, you will notice that the painted sun in it is slowly setting, and that every morning, without anyone seeing, it rises again.

Fin
