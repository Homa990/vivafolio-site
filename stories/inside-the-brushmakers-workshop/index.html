Inside the Brushmaker’s Workshop

Where wood remembers and brushes choose.

By Mara Kingsley · Frame-mender of Lantern Row

9-minute read by candlelight

A brush is only wood and hair, until it remembers whose hand it was waiting for.

There is a lane behind the fish market that does not appear on any map of the city, and I would not have found it at all if a wet black cat had not looked at me in a particular way and then walked into a wall. I followed it. The wall was not a wall. It was a narrow alley, so narrow that I had to turn sideways, and at the end of it there was a green door with no handle and a brass plate so worn that I could only read the last word: Brushes.

I knocked. The door opened before my knuckles had left it.

The brushmaker was smaller than I expected and older than seemed possible. His hands were as brown and knotted as willow roots, and his eyes were the clear, startling blue of a sky in a painting by someone who had never seen a real one. He did not ask who I was. He said, “You are late,” and stood aside.

Wood that remembers

Inside it smelled of linseed and beeswax and something older underneath, like a church after the candles have gone out. Brushes hung from every beam in bundles, thousands of them, and as I walked beneath them they stirred, all together, the way the grass stirs when a hawk passes over.

“They are curious,” the brushmaker said. “They do not get many visitors who are still alive.”

I laughed. He did not.

He showed me how it is done. The handles are cut only from willows that grow beside water, he said, because willow drinks, and a brush must be thirsty. The hairs are sable and badger and squirrel, gathered from creatures who gave them freely, combed from winter coats, picked from thorns. Each one is laid by hand, a single hair at a time, and as he worked he hummed a tune I almost knew, the kind of tune your mother hummed before you had words.

“And the brushes choose?” I asked, because that was what the old women of Lantern Row always said about his brushes, and I had always thought it was a figure of speech.

He put down his knife. “Come,” he said, “I will show you the drawer.”

At the back of the workshop was a cabinet of a hundred tiny drawers, each with a paper label written in a different hand. He opened one. Inside lay a single brush on a bed of faded velvet, and on the label: Agnes Hart, 1902, wanted to paint the sea.

“Every person who ever wanted to paint,” he said quietly, “and never did, leaves something behind when they die. A wish does not simply go out like a candle. It goes into the ground, and the willows drink it. I cut the willows. And the wish goes into the brush, and the brush remembers. It waits. Sometimes it waits a hundred years for the right hand: a child, a grandchild, a stranger with the same longing. And when that hand comes near, the brush knows.”

A wish does not simply go out like a candle. It goes into the ground, and the willows drink it.

The drawer with my name

I wish I could tell you that I was sensible and kept a careful distance. I was not, and I did not. Because as he spoke, one of the little drawers near the bottom of the cabinet began to rattle. Gently at first, then insistently, like a bird in a box.

The brushmaker looked at me for a long time. Then he knelt, and pulled it open, and lifted out a small round brush with a handle the colour of honey, and held out the label so I could read it.

Eleanor Kingsley, 1971. Wanted to paint her garden.

Eleanor Kingsley was my grandmother. She was a seamstress. She sewed other people’s wedding dresses for forty-two years, and her fingers were stiff with it by the end, and I never once saw her draw so much as a flower. But she had a garden, a small one, behind the house, with hollyhocks taller than me, and in the summer evenings she would sit out there until it was too dark to see, just looking at it, with an expression I did not have a word for when I was a child. I have a word for it now. It is longing.

The brush was trembling in his palm. Not shaking: reaching. Towards me.

“Every brush has a price,” the brushmaker said. “Its first painting is not yours. It belongs to the one it remembers. After that, it is yours for life.” He folded my fingers around the handle. It was warm, as if someone had only just put it down.

The first painting

I mend frames; I do not fill them. I went home that night and bought a child’s box of watercolours from the corner shop, and I sat at my kitchen table and painted hollyhocks, badly, from memory. My hand did not feel like my hand. It moved more slowly and more surely than it ever has, with a patience I do not possess, and when I was finished it was almost midnight and the painting was, I can say without vanity because it was not mine, beautiful.

I left it to dry. In the morning there was someone in it.

Standing among the hollyhocks, in a cotton dress I recognised from a photograph on my mother’s mantelpiece, was a young woman of about twenty with my grandmother’s eyes. She was not looking at the garden. She was looking out of the painting, at me, and she was smiling, and one hand was lifted as if she had just that moment finished waving.

I went back to the lane behind the fish market to thank him. There was no lane. There was a wall, a real one this time, of old soot-black brick, and on it, at about the height of my heart, a small brass plaque, freshly polished: Brushmaker. Closed 1887.

The brush is beside me as I write this. It is my brush now; the price has been paid. And some nights, when I cannot sleep, I hear the faint sound of it rattling in its jar, very gently, like something that has remembered someone else.

Fin
